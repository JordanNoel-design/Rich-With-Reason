What is blockchain? A beginner's explainer

Blockchain is the technology behind Bitcoin and most cryptocurrencies. Strip away the jargon and it's a shared record book that nobody can secretly change. Here's how it works.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • A blockchain is a shared digital record of transactions, copied across thousands of computers.
  • New records are bundled into "blocks" and linked in a chain, which makes past entries extremely hard to alter.
  • No single bank or company controls it. The network agrees on what's true using rules called consensus.

You've probably heard that blockchain will change everything, or that it's all hype. Before deciding either way, it helps to understand what it actually is. The core idea is simpler than the buzzwords suggest.

Start with a shared notebook

Imagine a group of friends who lend each other money. Instead of one person keeping track (who might make mistakes or cheat), everyone keeps an identical copy of the same notebook. When someone pays someone else, it's announced to the group and everyone writes it down. If one person tries to change their copy, it won't match everyone else's, and the group rejects it.

That's the essence of a blockchain: a shared, synchronized record that no single person controls.

How it works, step by step

  1. A transaction is requested. For example, Alex sends 0.01 bitcoin to Sam.
  2. It's broadcast to the network. Thousands of computers ("nodes") around the world receive it.
  3. It's checked. The network verifies that Alex actually has the funds and authorized the payment with a digital signature.
  4. It's bundled into a block with other recent transactions.
  5. The block is added to the chain. The network agrees the block is valid, and it's linked to the previous one.
  6. The record is permanent. Every node updates its copy. Sam now has the bitcoin.

Why is it called a "chain"?

Each block contains a unique digital fingerprint, called a hash, of the block before it. Change anything in an old block and its fingerprint changes, which breaks the link to every block after it. To rewrite history, an attacker would need to redo all those blocks and overpower the rest of the network at the same time. On a large blockchain, that's practically impossible.

Key features

  • Decentralized: No single company or government runs it.
  • Transparent: On public blockchains, anyone can view the transaction history.
  • Pseudonymous, not anonymous: Transactions are tied to wallet addresses, not names. But addresses can often be linked to real identities, especially through exchanges.
  • Hard to change: Once a transaction is confirmed, it's essentially permanent. There's no "undo" button and usually no customer service line.

What blockchains are used for

  • Cryptocurrencies: Bitcoin, Ethereum and thousands of others.
  • Stablecoins: digital tokens designed to hold a steady value, usually $1.
  • Smart contracts: programs on blockchains like Ethereum that run automatically when conditions are met. They power lending apps, exchanges and more.
  • Tokenized assets: representing things like funds or bonds as tokens.
  • Other uses being tested include supply-chain tracking and digital identity.

The limits and risks

  • Irreversible mistakes: Send crypto to the wrong address or to a scammer, and it's usually gone for good.
  • Security is your job: If you lose the keys to your wallet, no one can reset them.
  • Speed and cost: Some blockchains get slow or expensive when busy.
  • Not every problem needs one: For many tasks, a regular database run by a trusted company is simpler and faster.
  • Price swings: The technology can be sound while the coins built on it are extremely volatile.

The bottom line

A blockchain is a shared, tamper-resistant record kept by a network instead of one company. That makes it useful for moving value without a middleman, but it also means mistakes are permanent and security is up to you. Understanding the basics is the first step before investing a single dollar in crypto.
