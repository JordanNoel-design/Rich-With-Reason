Hot wallet vs. cold wallet

If you own crypto, how you store it matters as much as what you buy. Here's the difference between hot and cold wallets, and how to keep your coins safe.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • A crypto wallet doesn't hold coins. It holds the private keys that prove you own them.
  • Hot wallets are connected to the internet: convenient for spending, but more exposed to hacks.
  • Cold wallets keep keys offline: less convenient, but much safer for larger, long-term holdings.

In traditional banking, if someone steals your card, the bank can usually reverse the charges. In crypto, there's often no one to call. Whoever controls the keys controls the coins. That makes choosing and protecting your wallet one of the most important decisions you'll make.

First: what's in a wallet?

Your crypto actually "lives" on the blockchain. A wallet stores your private key, a long secret code that lets you authorize transactions. Most wallets also give you a recovery phrase (or seed phrase), usually 12 or 24 words, that can restore your wallet if your device is lost.

The golden rule: Anyone with your recovery phrase can take your crypto. No legitimate company, support agent or app will ever ask for it.

Custodial vs. self-custody

Before hot vs. cold, there's another choice: who holds the keys?

  • Custodial (exchange) accounts: The platform holds the keys for you. It's easy, with password resets and customer support, but you're trusting the company. If it's hacked or fails, your funds could be at risk. Crypto held this way isn't covered by FDIC insurance.
  • Self-custody wallets: You hold the keys. You have full control and full responsibility.

Hot wallets

A hot wallet is software connected to the internet: a mobile app, desktop program or browser extension.

Pros: free, fast and convenient for frequent transactions and for using crypto apps.

Cons: exposed to malware, phishing sites, fake apps and device theft.

Best for: small amounts you use regularly, like "spending money."

Cold wallets

A cold wallet keeps your private keys offline. The most common type is a hardware wallet, a small device that signs transactions without exposing your keys to the internet. Hardware wallets typically cost about $50 to $200.

Pros: much harder to hack remotely. Transactions require physical approval on the device.

Cons: costs money, less convenient, and you must protect both the device and your recovery phrase.

Best for: larger amounts and long-term holdings.

Connected to internetYesNo (keys stay offline)

Many people use both: a hot wallet as a checking account and a cold wallet as a savings vault.

Security checklist

  1. Write down your recovery phrase on paper, or stamp it into metal. Never store it in a photo, email, cloud note or password-free file.
  2. Keep copies in two secure places, like a home safe and a safe-deposit box.
  3. Buy hardware wallets directly from the manufacturer, never secondhand. Tampered devices are a known scam.
  4. Download wallet apps only from official sources. Fake apps are common.
  5. Use strong, unique passwords and an authenticator app (not SMS) for two-factor authentication on exchange accounts.
  6. Double-check addresses before sending. Some malware swaps copied addresses.
  7. Send a small test transaction first when moving large amounts.
  8. Be suspicious of "support" messages. Scammers often pose as wallet or exchange staff.

Plan for the unexpected

If something happened to you, could your family access your crypto? Without the keys, it may be lost forever. Consider documenting where your wallets are and how to access them, and talk with an estate-planning attorney about how to pass this information on safely.

The bottom line

Hot wallets are convenient, cold wallets are safer, and many people use both. Whatever you choose, your recovery phrase is the key to everything: keep it offline, keep it secret and never share it with anyone.
