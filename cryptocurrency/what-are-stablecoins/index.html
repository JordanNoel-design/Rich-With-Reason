What are stablecoins?

Stablecoins are cryptocurrencies designed to hold a steady value, usually $1. They've become a major part of the crypto world and now have their own U.S. law. Here's how they work and where the risks are.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • A stablecoin is a crypto token designed to stay worth a fixed amount, usually $1.
  • Most are backed by reserves of cash and short-term U.S. Treasury bills.
  • The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created the first federal rules for U.S. payment stablecoins. Even so, stablecoins aren't bank deposits and aren't FDIC-insured.

Most cryptocurrencies swing wildly in price, which makes them hard to use for everyday payments. Stablecoins solve that by pegging their value to a stable asset, usually the U.S. dollar. They're used to move money quickly around the world, trade on crypto exchanges and send payments any time of day.

How stablecoins keep their value

TypeHow it worksMain risk
Fiat-backedEach token is backed by $1 in reserves like cash and Treasury bills held by the issuerDepends on the issuer actually holding the reserves
Crypto-backedBacked by more than $1 of other crypto locked in smart contractsA sharp crypto crash can strain the system
AlgorithmicUses code and market incentives instead of full reservesCan collapse; several high-profile ones have

Fiat-backed stablecoins are by far the most widely used.

What people use them for

  • Trading: Parking money between crypto trades without converting back to dollars.
  • Payments and transfers: Sending dollars across borders in minutes, 24/7, often at low cost.
  • Access to dollars: In countries with unstable currencies, people use dollar stablecoins to protect their savings.
  • Crypto apps: Lending, borrowing and other blockchain-based services.

The GENIUS Act: new U.S. rules

In July 2025, the GENIUS Act became the first federal law focused on stablecoins. Key provisions include:

  • One-to-one reserves: Payment stablecoins must be fully backed by high-quality, liquid assets such as cash, insured deposits and short-term Treasury bills.
  • Licensed issuers: Issuers must be approved through bank subsidiaries, a federal license or a qualifying state license.
  • Disclosure: Issuers must regularly publish what's in their reserves.
  • No interest from the issuer: Issuers can't pay interest just for holding the stablecoin.
  • Priority in bankruptcy: Holders get first claim on reserves if an issuer fails.

Regulators spent 2026 writing the detailed rules, and most requirements are expected to take full effect by early 2027.

The risks to understand

  • Not FDIC-insured: A stablecoin is not a bank deposit. The government doesn't guarantee your balance if something goes wrong.
  • "Depegging": Stablecoins can temporarily trade below $1 during stress, as some did during the 2023 banking scare. Algorithmic stablecoins have collapsed entirely.
  • Issuer risk: Your stablecoin is only as good as the issuer's reserves and management.
  • Platform and wallet risk: Exchange failures, hacks, scams and lost keys apply here too.
  • Freezes: Many issuers can freeze tokens tied to illegal activity or court orders.
  • "Yield" offers: Platforms offering high interest on stablecoins are taking risks with your money. Ask exactly where the yield comes from.

Stablecoins vs. other options for your cash

StablecoinHigh-yield savings
Government insuranceNoYes, FDIC/NCUA up to $250,000
InterestNone from the issuer itselfYes, around 4% at top accounts in late 2026
Speed of transfersMinutes, 24/7, globalUsually 1–2 business days
Best useCrypto trading and fast transfersEmergency fund and savings

For your savings and emergency fund, an insured bank account is still the safer choice.

Are stablecoins taxable?

For tax purposes, stablecoins are generally treated like other crypto. Because their value stays near $1, most transactions create little or no gain. But you still need records, and any interest or rewards you earn are generally taxable income.

The bottom line

Stablecoins bring the stability of the dollar to blockchains, which makes them useful for payments and trading. New federal rules add important protections, but stablecoins still aren't insured bank deposits. Use them as a tool, not as a savings account.

Regulations described are current as of November 2026 and still being implemented. This article is for education only and isn't investment advice.
