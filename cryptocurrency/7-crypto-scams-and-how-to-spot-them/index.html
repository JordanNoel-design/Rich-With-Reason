7 crypto scams and how to spot them

Crypto fraud cost Americans more than $11 billion in 2025, according to the FBI. Most scams follow a few familiar scripts. Here's how to recognize them before it's too late.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20267 min read
The short version
  • Crypto-related fraud losses reported to the FBI reached about $11.4 billion in 2025, with investment scams the biggest category.
  • Red flags: guaranteed returns, pressure to act fast, strangers offering investment tips and requests for your recovery phrase.
  • Crypto payments are usually irreversible, so prevention is your best defense.

The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report found that crypto-related fraud accounted for more than half of all reported cybercrime losses in the U.S., with crypto investment scams alone costing about $7.2 billion. Scammers love crypto because payments are fast, global and almost impossible to reverse. Here are the seven scams to know.

1. Relationship investment scams ("pig butchering")

How it works: A stranger contacts you by text, social media or a dating app, often with a "wrong number" message. They build a friendship or romance over weeks, then mention how much they're making on a crypto platform. The site shows huge gains, but when you try to withdraw, you're told to pay "taxes" or "fees" first. The money is gone.

Red flag: Someone you've never met in person is giving you investment advice.

2. Fake investment platforms and apps

How it works: Professional-looking websites or apps promise guaranteed daily returns from "AI trading bots" or "arbitrage." Early small withdrawals may work to build trust.

Red flag: Guaranteed or unusually high returns. Real investments don't promise 2% a day.

3. Impersonation scams

How it works: Scammers pretend to be the government, police, your bank, a tech company or a utility. They claim there's a problem, such as a warrant, a hacked account or an unpaid bill, and tell you to "protect" your money by moving it into crypto, often through a crypto ATM.

Red flag: No legitimate agency or company will ever ask you to pay with crypto or a crypto ATM.

4. Phishing and fake support

How it works: You get a convincing email, text or social media message from "your exchange" or "wallet support," with a link to a fake login page or a request to "verify" your wallet by entering your recovery phrase.

Red flag: Anyone asking for your recovery phrase or private key. No real support team will ever request it.

5. Giveaway and celebrity scams

How it works: A post or video, sometimes a deepfake of a famous person, promises to "double any crypto you send."

Red flag: Free money in exchange for sending money first. It's always a scam.

6. Pump-and-dumps and "rug pulls"

How it works: Promoters hype a new token on social media. Prices spike as people buy in, then the creators sell everything or disappear with the funds, leaving buyers with worthless tokens.

Red flag: Heavy hype, influencer promotion, anonymous developers and pressure to "get in before it moons."

7. Recovery scams

How it works: After you've lost money, someone contacts you claiming they can recover it, sometimes posing as a lawyer or government agency, for an upfront fee.

Red flag: Anyone who contacts you offering to recover lost crypto for a fee. Victims often get scammed twice.

The universal warning signs
  • Guaranteed profits or "no risk"
  • Urgency: "act now," "limited time"
  • Requests for secrecy
  • Payment by crypto, crypto ATM or gift cards
  • Investment tips from someone you met online
  • Any request for your recovery phrase or private key

How to protect yourself

  1. Use well-known, regulated platforms, and type their web address yourself instead of clicking links.
  2. Never share your recovery phrase with anyone, for any reason.
  3. Turn on app-based two-factor authentication.
  4. Verify independently. If someone claims to be from a company or agency, hang up and contact them through their official website or number.
  5. Talk to someone you trust before sending money. Scammers rely on isolation and secrecy.
  6. Slow down. Real opportunities don't disappear in 30 minutes.

If you've been scammed

  1. Stop sending money, even if you're told it will unlock your funds.
  2. Report it to the FBI at ic3.gov and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  3. Contact the exchange you used to send the funds. It may be able to freeze accounts if it acts quickly.
  4. Save everything: messages, wallet addresses, transaction IDs and screenshots.
  5. Watch out for recovery scams that follow.

The bottom line

Crypto scams work because they feel personal, urgent and profitable. If anyone you haven't met in person pushes you toward a crypto investment, promises guaranteed returns or asks for your recovery phrase, stop. That pause could save you everything.

Loss figures come from the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report. This article is for education only.
