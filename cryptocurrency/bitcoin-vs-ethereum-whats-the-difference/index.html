Bitcoin vs. Ethereum: what's the difference?

They're the two biggest cryptocurrencies, but they were built for different jobs. Bitcoin aims to be digital money. Ethereum is a platform for building apps. Here's how they compare.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Bitcoin is designed as scarce digital money, with a fixed maximum supply of 21 million coins.
  • Ethereum is a programmable blockchain that runs apps and smart contracts. Its currency, ether (ETH), pays for using the network.
  • Both are highly volatile and risky. Neither is a substitute for an emergency fund or a diversified portfolio.

Bitcoin and Ethereum are often mentioned in the same breath, and both run on blockchains. But comparing them is a bit like comparing gold to a computer operating system. They share technology, not purpose.

Bitcoin: digital money

Bitcoin launched in 2009, created by an anonymous person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. Its goal: a currency that lets people send value directly to each other without banks.

  • Fixed supply: Only 21 million bitcoin will ever exist. New coins are released on a schedule that gets cut in half roughly every four years (the "halving").
  • Proof of work: Miners use computing power to secure the network, which uses a lot of energy.
  • Simple by design: Bitcoin deliberately limits what its blockchain can do, putting security and predictability first.
  • "Digital gold": Many supporters see it mainly as a store of value, a scarce asset some people hold as a hedge.

Ethereum: a world computer

Ethereum launched in 2015. Its key innovation is smart contracts: programs stored on the blockchain that run automatically when conditions are met.

  • A platform: Developers build apps for lending, trading, games, NFTs and stablecoins on Ethereum.
  • Ether (ETH): The network's currency, used to pay transaction fees (called "gas").
  • Proof of stake: In 2022 Ethereum switched from mining to staking, which cut its energy use dramatically.
  • No hard supply cap: New ETH is issued to validators, while some fees are "burned" (destroyed), so the supply can grow or shrink over time.

How people invest in them

  • Buying directly on a crypto exchange or app, then holding it there or in your own wallet.
  • Spot ETFs: U.S. exchange-traded funds that hold bitcoin or ether are available through regular brokerage accounts, including some IRAs. You get price exposure without managing a wallet, but you pay a yearly fee.
  • Staking ETH: Some holders lock up ether to help secure the network and earn rewards. Rewards are taxable, and staking adds risks such as lock-up periods and platform risk.

The risks are real

  • Extreme volatility: Both have fallen more than 50% from their peaks several times, sometimes within months.
  • No guaranteed value: Neither produces earnings or cash flow like a stock or bond. Prices depend on adoption and demand.
  • Regulation: Rules are still evolving and can move prices.
  • Security: Hacks, lost keys and scams cause permanent losses.

If you decide to invest

  1. Build your emergency fund and contribute to retirement accounts first.
  2. Invest only what you could afford to lose entirely. Many advisers suggest keeping crypto to a small slice of your portfolio, such as 5% or less.
  3. Stick to well-established coins and reputable platforms.
  4. Learn about wallet security before buying.
  5. Keep records for taxes. Selling or swapping crypto is usually a taxable event.

The bottom line

Bitcoin aims to be scarce digital money. Ethereum is a programmable platform with its own currency. Each has a different case for the future and different risks. Understand what you're buying, keep it small, and never invest money you'll need soon.

This article is for education only and isn't investment advice. Cryptocurrencies are highly volatile, and you could lose your entire investment.
