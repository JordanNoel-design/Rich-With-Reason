How crypto is taxed in the U.S.

The IRS treats crypto as property, so selling, swapping and even spending it can trigger taxes. Here's what's taxable, how it's calculated and what's new with Form 1099-DA.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20267 min read
The short version
  • The IRS treats crypto as property, not currency.
  • Selling, trading one coin for another or spending crypto can create a taxable capital gain or loss. Staking rewards, mining and crypto paychecks are taxed as income.
  • Brokers now report sales to the IRS on Form 1099-DA, and starting with 2026 transactions they'll report cost basis too.

Many crypto investors are surprised to learn that a simple swap from one coin to another can create a tax bill. Because the IRS treats digital assets as property, the rules work much like those for stocks, with a few important twists.

What's taxable and what isn't

ActivityTaxable?How
Buying crypto with dollars and holding itNo—
Moving crypto between your own walletsNoKeep records of the transfer
Selling crypto for dollarsYesCapital gain or loss
Trading one crypto for anotherYesCapital gain or loss
Spending crypto on goods or servicesYesCapital gain or loss
Getting paid in cryptoYesOrdinary income at fair market value
Staking rewards, mining, most airdropsYesOrdinary income when you gain control of them
Gifting cryptoUsually not to youLarge gifts may require a gift tax return

How capital gains are calculated

Gain or loss = sale price − cost basis
Your cost basis is generally what you paid, including fees.
Example: You bought 0.1 BTC for $5,000 and later sold it for $7,000. Your capital gain is $2,000.

Short-term vs. long-term

  • Short-term (held one year or less): taxed at your ordinary income tax rate, from 10% to 37%.
  • Long-term (held more than one year): taxed at lower rates of 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on your income.

Holding for more than a year can significantly cut your tax bill. High earners may also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax.

Losses can help

If you sell crypto for less than you paid, you have a capital loss. Losses offset gains, and up to $3,000 of extra losses a year can offset ordinary income. Anything beyond that carries forward to future years.

Under current law, the "wash sale" rule that applies to stocks doesn't apply to most crypto. That has let crypto investors sell at a loss and buy back quickly. Lawmakers have proposed changing this, so check the current rules before relying on it.

What's new: Form 1099-DA

  • 2025 transactions: Custodial crypto brokers, like major exchanges, began sending Form 1099-DA in early 2026, reporting gross proceeds from your sales to you and the IRS.
  • 2026 transactions: For assets bought on a platform on or after January 1, 2026 and sold there, brokers must also report your cost basis on forms sent in early 2027.
  • Not on the form? Crypto you bought before 2026 or moved in from another wallet may show proceeds with no cost basis. You're responsible for reporting the correct basis yourself.

The IRS can now match broker data to your return, so accurate records matter more than ever.

Where it goes on your tax return

  • Answer the digital asset question on the front of Form 1040.
  • Report sales and trades on Form 8949 and Schedule D.
  • Report crypto income (staking, mining, payments) as income, and on Schedule C if it's a business.

Keep good records

For every transaction, track the date, the amount, the value in dollars at the time, your cost basis and any fees. Crypto tax software can import exchange and wallet data and generate the forms. That's especially useful if you've traded across multiple platforms.

Ways to reduce crypto taxes legally

  1. Hold for more than a year to qualify for long-term rates.
  2. Harvest losses to offset gains.
  3. Use spot crypto ETFs in an IRA if your goal is long-term exposure. Gains inside tax-advantaged accounts aren't taxed each year.
  4. Donate appreciated crypto held over a year to a qualified charity. You may avoid capital gains and deduct its fair market value.
  5. Choose your accounting method carefully. Identifying specific lots can lower gains compared with the default first-in, first-out method, if your records support it.

The bottom line

Crypto taxes follow property rules: most sales, swaps and purchases are taxable, and income from staking or mining is taxed when received. With brokers now reporting to the IRS, keep detailed records and consider professional help if your activity is complex.

Tax rules are current as of November 2026 and may change. This article is for education only and isn't tax advice. Consult a qualified tax professional about your situation.
