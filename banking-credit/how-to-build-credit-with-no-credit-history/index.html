How to build credit with no credit history

No credit history means lenders can't score you yet. You can't skip that step, but you can get through it in months, not years.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • With no credit history, many scoring models can't produce a score for you at all. You're "credit invisible," not bad at credit.
  • The fastest ways to start are a secured credit card, a credit-builder loan or becoming an authorized user.
  • Pay on time and keep balances low, and a solid score can follow within about 6 to 12 months.

It's the classic catch-22: you need credit to get credit. Students, new arrivals to the U.S. and people who've always paid cash run into it all the time. The good news is that there are products built for exactly this situation, and they work.

Why you don't have a score yet

A credit score is a summary of your credit report. If your report is empty or too thin, there's nothing to summarize. Classic FICO models generally need at least one account that's been open for about six months and has been reported in the last six months. VantageScore can often score you sooner. Either way, the first step is the same: get an account reporting to the credit bureaus.

Five ways to start

1. A secured credit card

You put down a refundable deposit, often $200 to $500, and that usually becomes your credit limit. Otherwise it works like any credit card, and your payments are reported to the bureaus. Many issuers review your account after several months of on-time payments and may "graduate" you to a regular card and return your deposit.

Look for: no annual fee, reporting to all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) and a clear path to an upgrade.

2. A credit-builder loan

These are offered by many credit unions, community banks and some apps. The lender holds the loan amount in a savings account while you make fixed monthly payments. When you're done, you get the money back, minus any interest and fees. You build payment history and savings at the same time. It also adds an installment loan to your credit mix.

3. Become an authorized user

A family member or partner with good credit habits adds you to their credit card. If that issuer reports authorized users, the card's history can show up on your report. Only do this with someone who pays on time and keeps a low balance, because their mistakes can show up on your report too. You don't even need to use the card.

4. A starter or student card

Some unsecured cards are designed for people with limited history, especially students. They usually come with low limits. Approval isn't guaranteed, so check for "pre-qualification" tools, which use a soft inquiry that won't affect your credit.

5. Get credit for bills you already pay

Rent-reporting services and some bank-data tools can add rent, utilities or phone payments to your credit file. They don't count in every scoring model, but newer ones, including VantageScore 4.0, can use rent data when it's reported.

Avoid: Anyone who promises to "create a new credit identity" or sells you a "CPN" instead of your Social Security number. That's fraud, and you could be the one held responsible. Also be wary of paying big fees for "credit repair" when you don't have any credit yet.

Use your first account the right way

  1. Make a small purchase each month, like a streaming subscription or a tank of gas.
  2. Pay the full statement balance by the due date. You never need to carry a balance or pay interest to build credit. That's a myth.
  3. Keep your balance low. On a $300 limit, try to keep the reported balance under about $30 to $90.
  4. Turn on autopay so you never miss a payment. Payment history is the biggest part of your score.
  5. Don't apply for lots of cards at once. One or two accounts are enough to start.

A realistic timeline

  • Month 0: Open a secured card or credit-builder loan. Ask a family member about adding you as an authorized user.
  • Months 1 to 6: Small charges, paid in full and on time, every month.
  • Around 6 months: Most scoring models can now give you a score. Check it for free through your bank or card app.
  • Months 6 to 12: Ask about graduating your secured card. If you want a second account, consider one regular no-fee card.
  • Beyond: Keep your oldest account open and let time work for you.

Check your progress

You can pull your credit reports from all three bureaus for free every week at AnnualCreditReport.com. Make sure your new accounts appear and that the information is correct.

The bottom line

Starting from zero isn't a bad credit score. It's a blank page. Open one or two starter accounts, use them lightly and pay on time, every time. In a year you can have a credit history that opens the door to better cards, loans and apartments.

Product terms vary by lender. Confirm that any account you open reports to the credit bureaus. This article is for education only and isn't financial advice.
