Overdraft fees: how to avoid them for good

An overdraft fee can turn a $5 coffee into a $30 mistake. A few settings and habits can make sure it never happens to you again.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20265 min read
The short version
  • An overdraft happens when you spend more than is in your checking account and the bank covers it for a fee, often around $25 to $35.
  • You can opt out of overdraft coverage for debit card and ATM transactions. Then they're simply declined, with no fee.
  • Low-balance alerts, a small cushion and a bank with no overdraft fees take care of the rest.

Overdraft fees are among the most frustrating costs in banking because they hit when money is already tight. Banks still collect billions of dollars from them every year. The good news is that they're almost entirely avoidable once you know how they work.

How overdrafts work

If a payment comes through and your balance can't cover it, your bank has two choices:

  • Pay it anyway and charge you an overdraft fee. Your balance goes negative.
  • Reject it and possibly charge a non-sufficient funds (NSF) fee, which many banks have dropped.

Bankrate's most recent survey put the average overdraft fee at about $27, and many large banks still charge around $35. Some charge it more than once a day, and some add an extra fee if you stay negative for several days. A few small purchases on a bad day can add up to $100 or more in fees.

Step 1: Check your overdraft settings

Under federal rules, a bank can't charge you an overdraft fee on ATM withdrawals or one-time debit card purchases unless you've opted in to overdraft coverage. Many people opted in without realizing it when they opened their account.

Log in to your bank app or call the bank and ask: "Am I opted in to overdraft coverage for debit card and ATM transactions?" If you are, opt out. A card swipe without enough money will just be declined, which is mildly embarrassing but free.

Note: Opting out covers debit card and ATM transactions. Checks, automatic bill payments and other recurring charges can still overdraw your account, so the next steps matter too.

Step 2: Turn on low-balance alerts

Almost every bank app can send a text or push alert when your balance drops below an amount you choose. Set it to something like $100 or $200, enough warning to move money or hold off on spending.

Step 3: Link a backup account

Many banks offer overdraft protection transfers. If checking runs short, the bank pulls money from your linked savings account. The transfer fee, if any, is usually much smaller than an overdraft fee, and at many banks it's free. Ask how yours works.

Step 4: Keep a buffer

Treat a small amount, say $100 to $200, as "zero." Don't count it as spending money. That buffer absorbs the timing surprises: a subscription that renews early or a payment that clears before your paycheck lands.

Step 5: Line up your bill dates with payday

Many overdrafts come from timing, not overspending. Most companies will let you change a due date. Move your big bills to just after payday so the money is there when they're paid.

Step 6: Consider a bank that doesn't charge overdraft fees

A number of banks, including several large ones and many online banks, have dropped overdraft fees entirely. Others give you a grace period or a small "cushion" before any fee applies. If your bank still charges $35 per overdraft, switching may be the simplest fix of all.

Already got hit? Ask for a refund

If it's a first-time or rare overdraft, call your bank and politely ask them to waive the fee. Banks often refund one, especially for long-time customers. It takes five minutes and frequently works.

Watch out for the "cheap" fixes

Paycheck advance apps and payday loans can look like an easy way out of an overdraft. Some charge "tips" or express fees that work out to very high costs for a short loan. Use them rarely, if ever. A small emergency fund is the real long-term fix.

Your 10-minute checklist

  1. Opt out of debit and ATM overdraft coverage.
  2. Set a low-balance alert.
  3. Link your savings account for overdraft protection transfers.
  4. Pick your buffer amount and treat it as zero.
  5. Move bill due dates to right after payday.
  6. Compare your bank's fees with no-overdraft-fee banks.

The bottom line

Overdraft fees aren't a cost of having a bank account. They're a setting you can usually turn off, plus a couple of habits that keep the rest away. Spend 10 minutes on it today and you may never pay one again.

Common questions

Can I turn off overdraft protection?

You can opt out of overdraft coverage for debit card and ATM transactions, and your bank can't charge an overdraft fee on those unless you opted in. Those purchases will simply be declined instead.

Will my bank refund an overdraft fee?

Often, if it's a first-time or rare overdraft. Call, explain what happened and politely ask for a courtesy refund. Long-time customers in good standing usually have the best luck.

How much is an overdraft fee?

Bankrate's most recent survey put the average at about $27, and many large banks still charge around $35. Some banks have dropped overdraft fees entirely.

Overdraft policies vary by bank and change often. Check your account agreement for current terms. This article is for education only and isn't financial advice.
