How credit scores are actually calculated

Five ingredients, one three-digit number. Here's what goes into your FICO score, how much each part counts, and which levers actually move it.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Your FICO score is built from five factors: payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), credit mix (10%) and new credit (10%).
  • Paying on time and keeping your card balances low drive about two-thirds of your score.
  • Your income, savings and job aren't part of the formula at all.

A credit score can feel like a grade handed down by a mysterious committee. It's actually the output of a formula, and once you know the formula you know exactly where to put your effort and where you're wasting it.

First: which credit score?

You don't have one credit score. You have dozens. Two companies build the models most lenders use: FICO and VantageScore. Both read the data in your credit reports from the three bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion), and their main versions score you from 300 to 850.

FICO is still used in most lending decisions. VantageScore has been gaining ground, though. In 2026, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac began accepting VantageScore 4.0 for mortgages alongside classic FICO. That's why the score in your banking app may not match the one your lender pulls: it may be a different model, a different bureau or a different day. The good news is that both models reward the same habits.

The five ingredients of a FICO score

FactorWeight
Payment history35%
Amounts owed30%
Length of credit history15%
Credit mix10%
New credit10%

1. Payment history (35%)

This is the biggest piece, and it answers one question: do you pay what you owe, when you owe it? A payment usually isn't reported as late until it's at least 30 days past due. After that, each step (60 days, 90 days, collections) does more damage. A late payment can stay on your report for up to seven years, but its impact fades as it ages, especially if everything after it is on time.

Easiest win: Set up autopay for at least the minimum payment on every card and loan. Then pay the rest manually. One forgotten due date can cost you more points than months of good behavior earn.

2. Amounts owed (30%)

The key number here is your credit utilization: how much of your available credit you're using. If you have $5,000 in total card limits and $1,500 in balances, your utilization is 30%. Scoring models look at it overall and card by card.

The common advice is to stay under 30%, but people with the highest scores tend to sit in the single digits. One detail trips people up. Your card issuer usually reports your statement balance, not what you owe after you pay. So even if you pay in full every month, a big balance on statement day can show up as high utilization. Paying a chunk before your statement closes fixes that.

Utilization can also bounce back quickly. In classic FICO models, once a lower balance is reported, the score reflects it. Newer models such as VantageScore 4.0 also look at "trended data," meaning whether your balances have been rising or falling over time. That's another reason to pay balances down, not just move them around.

3. Length of credit history (15%)

This looks at the age of your oldest account, your newest account and the average age of all of them. You can't speed this one up. You can only avoid resetting it. That's why closing your oldest no-fee card is often a mistake. You lose its credit limit, which pushes utilization up. Over time you also lose its age once it eventually drops off your report.

4. Credit mix (10%)

Lenders like to see that you can handle different kinds of credit. That means revolving accounts like credit cards and installment loans like auto loans, student loans or a mortgage. It's a small factor. Never take out a loan just to improve your mix. The interest will cost far more than the few points it might add.

5. New credit (10%)

Each time you apply for credit, the lender usually does a hard inquiry. One inquiry typically costs only a few points. FICO counts it for 12 months, and it stays on your report for two years. Several new accounts in a short stretch can look risky, though. There's an exception for shoppers: multiple mortgage, auto or student loan inquiries within a short window are generally counted as one, so comparing rates won't hurt much. Checking your own score is a "soft" inquiry and never counts.

What's not in your score

Some things people assume matter don't show up at all. These include your income, savings balance, employer and job title, and age. Using a debit card doesn't count either, and neither does how often you check your own credit. Rent and utility payments usually don't count unless they're reported to the bureaus, though some newer models (including VantageScore 4.0) can use rent data when it's there.

A quick example

Maya and Luis both earn $60,000 and both have $5,000 in credit card limits.

  • Maya usually carries a $2,500 balance (50% utilization) and paid one bill 30+ days late last year.
  • Luis pays in full every month and his statements show about $300 (6% utilization).

Luis will almost certainly have the stronger score, by a wide margin, even though their income is identical. The two biggest factors, payment history and amounts owed, both favor him. Maya's fix doesn't need a raise. She needs autopay and a plan to bring that balance down.

Your 5-minute action plan

  1. Pull your reports. You can get free reports from all three bureaus every week at AnnualCreditReport.com, the official site. Dispute anything that isn't yours.
  2. Turn on autopay for at least the minimum on every account.
  3. Find your utilization. Divide your total balances by your total limits. If it's over 30%, pay down balances or make a payment before your statement date.
  4. Keep your oldest no-fee card open. Put a small recurring bill on it so the issuer doesn't close it for inactivity.
  5. Space out applications. When you're rate-shopping for a loan, do it within a couple of weeks.

The bottom line

About two-thirds of your FICO score comes down to two habits: paying on time and not using much of the credit you have. Get those right and most of the rest takes care of itself with time.

Common questions

What is a good credit score?

On the FICO scale of 300 to 850, 670 to 739 is considered good, 740 to 799 very good and 800 or higher exceptional. Lenders usually save their best rates for scores in the very good range and above.

How long do late payments stay on your credit report?

Up to seven years from the date the late payment was first reported. The damage fades as the late payment ages, especially if every payment after it is on time.

Does checking your own credit score lower it?

No. Checking your own score or report is a soft inquiry and never affects your score. Only hard inquiries, which happen when you apply for credit, can lower it a few points.

What is the fastest way to raise my credit score?

Pay down credit card balances so your utilization drops, ideally below 10% of your limits, and make every payment on time. Utilization changes can show up in your score as soon as the lower balance is reported.

Factor weights are FICO's published general guidelines; the exact impact of any action depends on your full credit profile and the scoring model a lender uses. This article is for education only and isn't financial advice.
