High-yield savings vs. money market accounts

They pay similar rates and carry the same insurance. The real difference is how you get your money out, and that should decide which one you open.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20265 min read
The short version
  • Both are insured savings accounts that pay far more than a typical big-bank savings account.
  • A money market account usually adds checks or a debit card. A high-yield savings account usually pays a little more and has lower minimums.
  • For most emergency funds, a high-yield savings account at an online bank is the simpler choice.

If your savings sit in the same bank as your checking account, there's a good chance they're earning almost nothing. Both high-yield savings accounts and money market accounts can fix that. They're closer cousins than their names suggest, so the choice comes down to a few practical details.

What they have in common

  • They're both bank deposits. At an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union, each is protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category.
  • Their rates are variable. The rate can rise or fall at any time, usually following the Federal Reserve.
  • They pay far more than average. In November 2026, the best high-yield savings accounts pay roughly 4% to 4.4% APY, while the FDIC's national average for savings is about 0.38%. On $10,000, that's around $400 a year versus less than $40.

The big practical difference is access. With most high-yield savings accounts, you move money to checking first and then spend it, which usually takes one to two business days. A money market account often lets you write a check or use a debit card straight from the account.

Don't mix these up: A money market account is a bank deposit and is FDIC- or NCUA-insured. A money market fund is an investment you buy through a brokerage. It's generally low-risk, but it isn't FDIC-insured. Both are useful; they're just not the same thing.

Which one should you choose?

Choose a high-yield savings account if…

  • You're building an emergency fund or saving for a goal like a car or a trip.
  • You want no minimum balance and no monthly fee.
  • A one- or two-day wait to reach your money is fine, or even helpful because it keeps you from dipping in.

Choose a money market account if…

  • You keep a larger balance and want to pay some big bills straight from savings, like a property tax bill or a contractor.
  • You can meet the minimum to earn the top rate.
  • You like having a checkbook for that money without parking it in a low-paying checking account.

What to check before you open either one

  1. The real APY and its conditions. Some top rates are promotional, last only a few months or apply only up to a set balance. Read the fine print.
  2. Fees and minimums. A $10 monthly fee can wipe out the interest on a small balance.
  3. Insurance. Confirm the bank is FDIC-insured or the credit union is NCUA-insured. Some apps hold your money at partner banks, so check which one.
  4. Withdrawal limits. The federal six-per-month limit was removed in 2020, but some banks still cap transfers or charge for extras.
  5. Transfer speed. Find out how long it takes to move money to your checking account.

A simple setup that works for most people

Keep about one month of spending in checking. Put your emergency fund in a high-yield savings account at a separate online bank, so it isn't sitting one tap away. Then set up an automatic transfer on payday. If you later build a large cash cushion and want check-writing, a money market account can hold part of it.

The bottom line

The difference between these two accounts is small. The difference between either one and a 0.4% savings account is huge. Pick the one that fits how you'll use the money, and then automate your deposits.
