Balance transfer cards: when they're worth it

A 0% balance transfer can save you more than a thousand dollars in interest, or cost you a fee for nothing. Here's how to tell which one you're about to get.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • A balance transfer moves debt from a high-interest card to a new card with 0% APR for a set time, usually for a 3% to 5% fee.
  • It pays off when you can clear most or all of the balance before the 0% period ends.
  • It backfires if you keep adding new debt or miss a payment.

Credit card interest is brutal. Most regular card APRs today run from the high teens to the high twenties. A balance transfer card hits pause on that interest so more of every payment goes toward what you actually owe. Used well, it's one of the best debt tools there is. Used carelessly, it just moves the problem around.

How a balance transfer works

  1. You apply for a card with a 0% intro APR on balance transfers. Today the longest offers run up to about 21 months.
  2. Once you're approved, you ask the new card to pay off your old card's balance, usually up to your new credit limit.
  3. The new card adds a balance transfer fee, typically 3% to 5% of the amount moved.
  4. You pay 0% interest on that balance until the intro period ends. After that, the card's regular APR applies to whatever is left.

The math: when it's worth it

Say you owe $6,000 at 24% APR and can pay $300 a month.

  • Stay put: It takes about 26 months to pay off, and you pay roughly $1,740 in interest.
  • Transfer to a 21-month 0% card with a 5% fee: The fee is $300, so you owe $6,300. At $300 a month, it's gone in exactly 21 months. Your total cost is $300.

That's about $1,440 saved, and you're debt-free five months sooner. With a 3% fee the savings are even bigger.

Quick test: Divide your balance (plus the fee) by the number of 0% months. If you can comfortably pay that amount every month, a transfer is very likely worth it. If you can only pay half of it, you'll still save, but have a plan for what's left when the regular APR kicks in.

When it's not worth it

  • The balance is small. If you can pay off $800 in two or three months anyway, the fee may cost more than the interest you'd save.
  • You'll keep using the old card. If the old card fills right back up, you end up with two balances instead of one.
  • Your credit isn't strong enough. The best offers usually need good to excellent credit, roughly 670 or higher. A smaller credit limit may only cover part of your debt.
  • The real problem is overspending. A transfer buys time. It doesn't fix a budget that runs short every month.

The fine print that trips people up

  • Transfer deadlines. Many cards only give the 0% rate on transfers made within a set window after opening, often 60 to 120 days.
  • Same-bank rule. You usually can't move a balance between two cards from the same issuer.
  • New purchases. Some cards give 0% on transfers but not on purchases. New spending could start charging interest right away. Keep the card for the transfer only.
  • Late payments. On many cards, one late payment can end the 0% rate early. Set up autopay for at least the minimum.
  • Minimums aren't a plan. The required minimum payment is usually far too small to clear the balance in time. Set your own payment using the quick test above.

What happens to your credit score?

Applying adds a hard inquiry and a new account, which may dip your score a few points at first. Over time, a transfer often helps. Your total available credit goes up, and as you pay the balance down, your utilization falls. Utilization is one of the biggest factors in your score. Keep the old card open, with a zero balance, so you don't lose that available credit.

A step-by-step game plan

  1. Add up the balances and APRs on every card.
  2. Compare the 0% period length, the transfer fee and the regular APR after the intro period.
  3. Do the quick test to work out your monthly payment.
  4. Apply, then complete the transfer before the deadline.
  5. Set up autopay for your planned payment, not just the minimum.
  6. Put the old card somewhere out of reach, but keep it open.
  7. Put a reminder in your calendar a few months before the 0% period ends.

The bottom line

A balance transfer is worth it when three things are true: the interest you'll avoid is much bigger than the fee, you can pay off most of the balance before the 0% window closes, and you've stopped adding new debt. If all three are true, it's one of the fastest ways out of credit card debt.

Card terms such as intro periods, fees and APRs vary and change often. Check the current offer details before applying. Example figures are rounded estimates. This article is for education only and isn't financial advice.
