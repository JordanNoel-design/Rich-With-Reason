Tax refunds vs. adjusting your withholding

A big tax refund feels like a bonus, but it's really your own money coming back late. Here's how to decide whether to keep the refund or put that money in your paycheck instead.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • A refund means you overpaid taxes during the year. The IRS returns it without interest.
  • Adjusting your W-4 can move that money into your paychecks, which could mean a few hundred dollars more each month.
  • A refund isn't wrong. It's a trade-off between convenience and control.

Through mid-April of the 2026 filing season, the average federal refund was about $3,400, according to IRS data. For many households it's the biggest single deposit of the year. But it's worth asking: is a giant refund actually the best way to get that money?

How withholding works

When you start a job, you fill out a Form W-4. Your employer uses it to estimate how much federal income tax to take out of each paycheck. At tax time, you compare what was withheld with what you actually owe:

  • Withheld more than you owe → you get a refund.
  • Withheld less than you owe → you owe a balance, and possibly a penalty if it's large.

A refund isn't a gift. It's money you lent the government for free.

The case for adjusting your withholding

  • More money every month. A $3,000 refund is about $250 a month, or $115 per paycheck if you're paid every two weeks.
  • Put it to work right away. That money could pay down a credit card charging 20%+ interest, build your emergency fund or go into investments.
  • Less temptation. Big refunds often get spent on impulse. Smaller monthly amounts can go into a planned budget.
  • Less reliance on credit. Extra cash flow means you're less likely to put surprises on a card.

The case for keeping your refund

  • Forced savings. If extra money in your paycheck would just disappear, a refund guarantees a lump sum once a year.
  • A goal for the lump sum. Some people plan around it to pay off a debt, fund a vacation or top up an emergency fund.
  • Peace of mind. No risk of an unexpected bill in April.
  • Refundable credits. If most of your refund comes from credits like the Earned Income Tax Credit or the refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit, it isn't overwithholding. Those credits are paid out when you file.
Middle ground: Aim for a small refund, a few hundred dollars. You keep most of the money in your paycheck but still have a cushion against owing.

Why your refund might change this year

Tax law changes in 2025 added several new deductions, including temporary deductions for some tip and overtime income, a bigger deduction for seniors and a deduction for some car loan interest. These helped push average refunds higher in 2026. If you qualify for any of them, your withholding may now be higher than it needs to be. It's worth a fresh check.

How to adjust your withholding

  1. Grab your latest pay stub and last year's tax return.
  2. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov. It's free and walks you through your situation.
  3. Fill out a new W-4 based on the results and give it to your employer, usually through your HR or payroll portal.
  4. Check your next pay stub to confirm the change took effect.
  5. Re-check after big life changes: marriage, divorce, a new baby, a second job or a big raise.

Common W-4 situations

  • Two jobs, or both spouses work: These households are the most likely to under-withhold. Use the estimator or the "Multiple Jobs" step on the W-4.
  • Side income: If you have freelance or gig income, you can have extra tax withheld from your main job's paycheck instead of making separate quarterly payments.
  • Big deductions or credits: If you expect large deductions, the W-4 lets you account for them so less is withheld.

Don't overcorrect

Withholding too little can leave you with a bill in April and possibly an underpayment penalty. In general, you can avoid a penalty if you've paid at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% if your income is higher). Make small adjustments and check them.

What to do with the extra paycheck money

Adjusting only helps if the money goes somewhere useful. Before you change your W-4, decide where the extra will go and automate it:

  1. Pay down high-interest debt.
  2. Build your emergency fund.
  3. Increase your retirement contributions.
  4. Fund your sinking funds.

The bottom line

If you're disciplined with money, adjusting your withholding gives you control of your cash all year. If you'd rather have a forced savings plan, a refund works, just know what it is. Either way, run the IRS estimator once a year so the choice is yours, not an accident.

Tax rules change and individual situations vary. Consider talking with a tax professional. This article is for education only and isn't tax advice.
