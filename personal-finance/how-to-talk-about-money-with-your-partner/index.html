How to talk about money with your partner

Money is one of the most common sources of tension in relationships, and one of the best ways to build trust. Here's how to start the conversation and keep it going.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Start with goals and values, not numbers or blame.
  • Share the full picture: income, debts, credit and spending habits.
  • Pick a system for combining money that feels fair to both of you, then check in regularly.

Many couples avoid talking about money until something goes wrong: a surprise bill, a hidden debt or an argument over a purchase. But couples who talk openly about money tend to make better decisions and feel more like a team. Here's how to have the conversation without it turning into a fight.

Why money talks feel hard

Money is never just money. It's tied to how you grew up, what you fear and what you dream about. One partner may have grown up watching parents struggle and now feels anxious about spending. The other may have learned that money is meant to be enjoyed. Neither is wrong. Understanding why you each feel the way you do makes the numbers much easier to discuss.

Before the conversation

  • Choose the right time. Not when you're tired, rushed or already arguing. Schedule it like a date: coffee on a Saturday morning works well.
  • Set the tone. Say it's about working as a team, not judging each other.
  • Do a little prep. Each of you can jot down your income, debts and rough monthly spending, plus one or two things you'd love to do with money.

Start with these questions

Begin with values and goals before you get to the spreadsheets:

  1. What did money look like in your home growing up?
  2. What does "financial security" mean to you?
  3. What's one thing you'd love to do or have in the next five years?
  4. What's your biggest money worry right now?
  5. What's a purchase you think is worth spending a lot on? What feels wasteful to you?

Listen more than you talk. You're trying to understand, not to win.

Then share the full picture

Once you trust each other, put everything on the table:

  • Income from every source
  • Debts: student loans, credit cards, car loans, money owed to family
  • Credit scores
  • Savings and retirement accounts
  • Regular spending and subscriptions
Be honest about debt. Hidden debt is one of the most damaging forms of "financial infidelity." It's much better to share it early, with a plan, than to have it discovered later.

Choose how to combine your money

There's no single right answer. Common setups include:

SystemHow it worksGood for
All jointAll income goes into shared accountsCouples with fully shared goals and similar habits
Yours, mine and oursA joint account for shared bills plus separate personal accountsMost couples: shared goals with some independence
Fully separateEach pays an agreed share of costsNew relationships, or couples who strongly value independence

For uneven incomes, many couples split shared costs in proportion to income. If one partner earns 60% of the household income, they cover 60% of shared bills. That often feels fairer than a 50/50 split.

Set ground rules

  • A "check-in" amount: Agree that any purchase over a set amount ($200? $500?) gets discussed first.
  • Personal spending money: Each person gets an amount to spend with no questions asked. This prevents a lot of arguments.
  • Shared goals: Pick one or two to work on together, like an emergency fund, a vacation or paying off a credit card.

Keep the conversation going

One talk isn't enough. Set up a regular money date, monthly or every couple of weeks, for about 30 minutes:

  1. Celebrate one win since last time.
  2. Review spending against your plan.
  3. Look at upcoming expenses.
  4. Check progress on your shared goals.
  5. Decide on one thing to adjust.

Keep it short and positive. Order takeout or pour a glass of wine. Make it something you look forward to.

When you disagree

  • Focus on the specific decision, not your partner's character. Say "I'm worried about this purchase," not "You're bad with money."
  • Look for the goal underneath: security, freedom, fun, status.
  • Try a compromise, like a trial period or splitting the difference.
  • If money arguments keep repeating, a financial counselor or couples therapist can help.

The bottom line

Talking about money is a skill, and like any skill it gets easier with practice. Start with values, share the full picture, pick a fair system and check in regularly. The goal isn't to agree on everything. It's to make decisions as a team.

Common questions

When should couples talk about money?

Before big commitments like moving in, getting engaged or combining accounts, and then regularly. A short monthly money date keeps small issues from becoming big ones.

Should couples combine finances?

There's no single right answer. Many couples use a joint account for shared bills plus separate personal accounts. Others combine everything or keep everything separate. Pick what feels fair to both of you.

How do couples split bills with different incomes?

Many split shared costs in proportion to income. If one partner earns 60% of the household income, they cover 60% of shared bills, which often feels fairer than 50/50.

If you're in a relationship where money is used to control or limit you, that's a form of financial abuse. Support is available through the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233. This article is for education only.
