What does 'Section 301' mean?

The U.S. just proposed new Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners, from China to the EU. Here's what Section 301 means, how the process works, who pays tariffs and what it could mean for your money.

By Jordan NoelNov 26, 20265 min read
The short version
  • Section 301 is a part of U.S. trade law that lets the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) respond, often with tariffs, when another country's policies are found to be unfair and to hurt American businesses.
  • It's in the news because on November 25, 2026, the USTR proposed new Section 301 tariffs on goods from 14 economies, including China, the European Union, Japan, India and Vietnam.
  • The tariffs are only proposed. Public comments are due January 5, 2027, and nothing has taken effect yet.

If "Section 301" showed up in your news feed this week, here's why. On November 25, the USTR announced findings in 14 trade investigations and proposed tariffs as high as 35%. The same day, stocks swung sharply, with the S&P 500 down as much as 3.2% before closing 0.3% lower, as investors weighed AI stocks, inflation and plans to escalate the trade war, the Associated Press reported.

What is a Section 301 tariff?

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 is one of the laws that lets the U.S. government impose one. According to the Congressional Research Service, it allows an investigation when a foreign government's act, policy or practice is "unjustifiable" and "burdens or restricts" U.S. commerce, or when U.S. rights under a trade agreement are being denied.

The USTR, the agency that leads U.S. trade policy, runs the process. If it finds a problem, the remedies can include tariffs, other import limits or a binding agreement with the other country.

How the process works

  • An investigation opens. The USTR can start one itself or respond to a petition, and it consults with the foreign government involved.
  • The public weighs in. Businesses, workers and other groups file comments and testify at hearings.
  • The USTR makes a determination. CRS says this generally happens within 12 months.
  • Action follows. If the finding is affirmative, the USTR can impose tariffs or other measures. CRS notes these actions end after four years unless they are reviewed and continued.

Why Section 301 is in the news now

The current investigations began on March 11, 2026, when the USTR opened cases on 16 economies over "structural excess capacity" in manufacturing: the concern that some countries' policies lead factories to produce far more than their own markets need.

On November 25, it made determinations for 14 of them. Mexico and Norway were not included. The proposed rates include:

  • Up to 15%: the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan.
  • Up to 18%: Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand.
  • Up to 20%: Singapore, and China on goods not listed in special annexes. Some Chinese goods face up to 10%, and others are exempt.
  • Up to 25%: India. Up to 35%: Vietnam.

The USTR says the rates for Singapore, India and Vietnam could be lower if agreements are reached. The law firm Wiley stresses that the tariffs "are proposed measures and are not yet in effect," in its summary. Written comments are due January 5, 2027, and hearings start January 25, 2027.

Section 301 vs. IEEPA tariffs: what's the difference?

Section 301 matters more this year because of a court ruling. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) "does not authorize the President to impose tariffs," according to DLA Piper's summary. That struck down the so-called reciprocal tariffs. Tariffs under Sections 232 and 301 were not affected. Euronews reported that the new duties would help replace revenue lost after the ruling.

The key difference is process. IEEPA is an emergency powers law. Section 301 tariffs come only after an investigation, a determination and a public comment period.

A short history: the 2018 China tariffs

Section 301 is best known for the trade war with China. In 2018, the USTR imposed tariffs ranging from 7.5% to 25% on around $370 billion worth of U.S. imports from China, CRS reports.

Who pays Section 301 tariffs?

Research on the 2018 tariffs found that the costs were "largely passed through" to U.S. prices, the National Bureau of Economic Research summarized. A follow-up study by economists Mary Amiti, Stephen Redding and David Weinstein found the tariffs "continue to be almost entirely borne by U.S. firms and consumers." Results for the new tariffs could differ, and they depend on rates that aren't final.

What it means for everyday readers

  • Nothing changes at the register yet. These are proposals. Watch for final decisions after the January hearings.
  • Some prices could rise later. If imported goods get pricier, a flexible budget helps. Our guide to cutting monthly bills is a good place to start.
  • Keep a cushion. Trade news can move prices and jobs in some industries. A solid emergency fund gives you room.
  • Don't trade on headlines. Markets swing on trade news, as Wednesday showed. A diversified plan, like a three-fund portfolio, is built to ride that out.

Related buzzwords

  • Excess capacity: when factories can make far more than buyers want, often pushing prices down and exports up.
  • Section 232: a different trade law that allows tariffs on national security grounds.

The bottom line

Section 301 is the trade law the U.S. uses to answer what it sees as unfair foreign practices. The new tariffs are proposals, not final, and the public comment period runs into January. For your money, the takeaway is simple: don't panic over headlines, keep your budget flexible and stay diversified.

Common questions

What is a Section 301 tariff?

A Section 301 tariff is a tax on imports that the U.S. Trade Representative can impose under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. It is used when an investigation finds that a foreign government's act, policy or practice is unjustifiable and burdens or restricts U.S. commerce.

Are the new Section 301 tariffs in effect?

No. The tariffs the USTR announced on November 25, 2026, for 14 economies are proposals. Written comments are due January 5, 2027, and public hearings begin January 25, 2027.

Who pays Section 301 tariffs?

Research on the 2018 tariffs on Chinese goods found the costs were largely passed through to U.S. prices. A 2020 study by Amiti, Redding and Weinstein found the tariffs were almost entirely borne by U.S. firms and consumers.

What is the difference between Section 301 and IEEPA tariffs?

Section 301 tariffs follow an investigation, a determination and a public comment period run by the USTR. In February 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that IEEPA, an emergency powers law, does not authorize the President to impose tariffs; Section 301 tariffs were not affected.
