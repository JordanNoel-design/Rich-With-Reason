How much should be in your emergency fund?

Three months? Six? A year? The right answer depends on how stable your income is and who depends on you. Here's how to find your number and build it.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Start with a $1,000 to $2,000 starter fund, then build up to 3 to 6 months of essential expenses.
  • Aim higher, 6 to 12 months, if your income is irregular, you're the only earner or you work in an unstable industry.
  • Keep it in a high-yield savings account that's separate from your checking account.

An emergency fund is the most boring part of personal finance, and the most important. It's what turns a job loss, a car repair or a surprise medical bill from a crisis into an inconvenience. Without one, emergencies usually land on a credit card at 20%+ interest, and the debt can follow you for years.

Step 1: Calculate your essential monthly expenses

Your emergency fund should cover what you must pay, not your full lifestyle. Add up:

  • Rent or mortgage
  • Utilities, phone and internet
  • Groceries
  • Transportation
  • Insurance premiums
  • Minimum debt payments
  • Childcare and essential medical costs

Leave out dining out, travel and shopping. In a real emergency, those get cut first.

Step 2: Pick your number of months

Your situationTarget
Stable salaried job, dual income, no kids3 months
Stable job, single income or kids4–6 months
Commission, freelance, gig or seasonal income6–12 months
Self-employed or business owner6–12 months
Older home or older car, health issuesAdd 1–2 months
Near retirement or in a specialized field6+ months

Ask yourself: If I lost my income tomorrow, how long would it realistically take to replace it? That's a good guide to how many months you need.

Step 3: Do the math

Example: Marcus has essential expenses of $3,200 a month. He has a stable job, but he's the only earner for his family of three, so he picks 5 months. His target: $3,200 × 5 = $16,000.

If that number feels huge, that's normal. Nobody builds it overnight. Break it into milestones.

Build it in stages

  1. Starter fund: $1,000 to $2,000. This covers the most common emergencies, like car repairs, an urgent vet bill or a broken appliance. Build this first, even while paying down debt.
  2. One month of essentials. Now a short gap in income won't wreck you.
  3. Three months. You're protected against most job changes and medical surprises.
  4. Your full target. Then shift your savings toward investing and other goals.

Where to keep it

Your emergency fund has two jobs: be safe and be available. Earning a return is a distant third.

  • Best: A high-yield savings account at an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union. The top accounts paid around 4% APY in late 2026, far above the national average.
  • Also fine: A money market account. For a large fund, consider a mix, such as a "CD ladder" or Treasury bills for the portion you're unlikely to need right away.
  • Avoid: Stocks or crypto. Emergencies and market crashes often arrive together, such as when layoffs hit during a recession.

Keep it at a different bank from your checking account. One or two days of transfer time is just enough friction to stop "emergency" pizza.

What counts as an emergency?

A simple test: is it unexpected, necessary and urgent?

  • ✓ Job loss or reduced hours
  • ✓ Medical or dental bills
  • ✓ Essential car or home repairs
  • ✓ Emergency travel for family
  • ✗ Holiday gifts, vacations or annual insurance premiums. These are predictable, so plan for them with sinking funds instead.

How to build it faster

  • Automate it. Set a transfer for payday, even $25 or $50.
  • Use windfalls. Put tax refunds, bonuses or side hustle income straight in.
  • Cancel and redirect. Cut one or two subscriptions and send that money to savings.
  • Round up. Many banks offer round-up features that move spare change into savings.

Should you invest or build the emergency fund first?

A common order: (1) build a starter fund, (2) get your full employer 401(k) match, (3) pay off high-interest debt, (4) finish your full emergency fund, and (5) invest more. The match is free money, so don't skip it while you build savings.

After you use it

That's exactly what it's for, so don't feel guilty. Once the emergency is handled, pause extra goals and rebuild the fund before moving on.

The bottom line

Start with $1,000, work toward 3 to 6 months of essential expenses and keep the money safe and separate. It won't make you rich, but it will protect everything else you're building.
