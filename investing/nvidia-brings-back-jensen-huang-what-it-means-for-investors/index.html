Nvidia brings back Jensen Huang: what it means for investors

Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO less than two weeks after he resigned, and the stock jumped before the bell. Here's what happened, how markets reacted, and what key person risk means for your money.

By Jordan NoelNov 23, 20265 min read
The short version
  • Nvidia's board brought back Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately, less than two weeks after accepting his resignation.
  • Investors cheered. Nvidia shares were up about 27% before the market opened on Monday, November 23, and stock futures rose broadly.
  • The episode is a real-world lesson in key person risk: how much a company's value can depend on one leader.

Few companies have had a stranger two weeks than Nvidia. Its longtime CEO left, its stock fell hard, and then the board asked him back. Here's what happened, how markets reacted, and what it means for everyday investors.

Why did Jensen Huang resign?

On November 11, Nvidia's board accepted Huang's resignation as president, CEO and director, effective immediately, according to the company's SEC filing. The company's announcement gave no reason. It named Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress interim CEO, made director Harvey C. Jones the board's first-ever chair and said it would search for a permanent CEO.

Reuters reported that Huang resigned after revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with a businesswoman U.S. authorities suspect of spying for China, an allegation Beijing denies.

Is Jensen Huang back as Nvidia's CEO?

Yes. On November 22, the board reinstated Huang as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately, according to an SEC filing released Monday. Reuters called it a sharp reversal from the board's decision just days earlier to rule out his return.

In its press release, Nvidia said:

  • The vote was unanimous. The board said it was "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead NVIDIA."
  • Washington weighed in. The board said it "received input from senior levels of the United States government" and is confident his return "poses no national security risk."
  • The investigation continues. A committee of independent directors, working with independent counsel, will see its investigation through to the end. Huang said he fully supports it.
  • New guardrails. Nvidia is creating a National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors and is searching for a chief compliance officer. Jones stays on as chair, and Kress continues as CFO.

Why is Nvidia stock up today?

Because investors are betting the company is better off with its founder in charge. Before the bell, Nvidia was up 27% at $159.48 as of 9 a.m. ET, 24/7 Wall St. reported. Premarket prices can change sharply once regular trading starts.

That bounce follows a steep slide. According to Yahoo Finance price data:

  • Nvidia closed at $173.73 on November 11, the day the board accepted Huang's resignation.
  • It closed at $125.53 on Friday, November 20, down about 28% from November 11. Friday alone it fell 14%.
  • Its October 6 close was $239.24, so even after Monday's early jump the stock would need to rise about 50% to get back there.

Not all of that drop was about Huang. Friday's selloff came after Reuters reported that the SEC was probing Nvidia's accounting, and the whole AI trade was under pressure. Nvidia's release says the company "will continue to cooperate with ongoing government investigations and inquiries."

How did the broader market react?

The relief spread. By 7:29 a.m. ET, Nasdaq 100 futures were up 3.1%, S&P 500 futures 2.5% and Dow futures 1.8%, Investing.com reported. That came after a brutal Friday, when the S&P 500 fell 3.7% to 6,910.39 and the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3% to 22,617.73. Deutsche Bank strategists wrote that "a lot of bad news is already being priced."

One morning doesn't make a trend. The AI selloff had several causes, and the worries about AI financing haven't gone away.

What is key person risk?

Key person risk is the danger that a company loses value because it depends heavily on one person, often a founder or CEO. When that person leaves suddenly, investors have to guess how the business will run without them. Nvidia's two weeks show both sides: the stock fell after Huang left and jumped when he came back.

What it means for everyday investors

  • Single stocks can swing wildly. Nvidia lost more than a quarter of its value in under two weeks. That's why a diversified portfolio beats betting on one company.
  • You probably own some Nvidia already. Broad U.S. index funds hold it, so its swings show up in your account even if you never bought it directly.
  • Don't chase headlines. Buying after a 27% jump, or selling after a 14% drop, means trading on news that's already priced in. Steady investing, like dollar-cost averaging, takes the guesswork out.
  • Watch governance, not just growth. Boards, succession plans and compliance matter, and Nvidia's new committees are a sign investors noticed.

The bottom line

Nvidia's board reversed course and brought Jensen Huang back, and the stock surged before Monday's open. But the investigation continues, the AI trade is still shaky, and the stock remains far below its October level. For most people, the lesson is the one markets keep teaching: diversify, think long term, and don't let one company's drama run your plan.
