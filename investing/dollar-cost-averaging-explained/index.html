Dollar-cost averaging, explained

Investing the same amount on a regular schedule takes emotion out of the market. Here's how dollar-cost averaging works, what the research says, and when investing all at once is better.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20265 min read
The short version
  • Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule, no matter what the market is doing.
  • If you invest from each paycheck, you're already doing it, and that's a great habit.
  • If you have a lump sum, investing it all at once has historically come out ahead about two-thirds of the time, but DCA can reduce regret.

Trying to "buy low and sell high" sounds simple. In practice, almost nobody can predict when the market will rise or fall. Dollar-cost averaging sidesteps the problem completely: you stop trying to time the market and simply invest on a schedule.

How it works

Say you invest $200 on the first of every month into an index fund. When prices are high, your $200 buys fewer shares. When prices drop, the same $200 buys more shares.

You bought 21 shares for $800, an average cost of about $38.10 a share, which is lower than the average market price of $41.25. You automatically bought more when prices were low. Once the price recovered to $50, your shares were worth $1,050.

The benefits

  • Removes emotion. You don't have to decide whether "now is a good time." Fear and excitement both lead to bad decisions.
  • Turns downturns into opportunities. Market drops mean your regular investment buys more.
  • Builds a habit. Investing becomes automatic, like paying a bill.
  • Fits how most people earn. Investing a set amount from each paycheck is natural.

DCA vs. investing a lump sum all at once

This is where it gets interesting. If you already have a large sum, such as an inheritance, a bonus or money from selling a house, should you invest it all now or spread it out?

Vanguard studied this using market data from 1976 to 2022. Investing a lump sum right away beat spreading it out over a year about 68% of the time. The reason is simple: markets rise more often than they fall, so money waiting on the sidelines usually misses gains.

But DCA beat holding the money in cash most of the time, and it does better during the less common periods when markets fall soon after you invest.

So which should you choose? If you can handle seeing your investment drop soon after you buy, investing the lump sum has better odds. If a big early drop would make you panic and sell, or keep you from investing at all, spreading it over 3 to 12 months is a perfectly reasonable compromise.

Common questions

How often should I invest?

Matching your pay schedule (every two weeks or monthly) works well. The exact frequency matters far less than being consistent.

Should I stop during a market crash?

No. That's when DCA does its best work, because you're buying more shares at lower prices. Stopping during a downturn defeats the purpose.

Does DCA guarantee a profit?

No. It reduces the risk of investing everything at a bad moment, but it can't protect you if the investment itself falls over the long term. That's why it works best with diversified funds.

What should I DCA into?

Broad, low-cost index funds or target-date funds are ideal. DCA into a single risky stock still carries a lot of single-stock risk.

How to set it up

  1. Choose your account: a 401(k), IRA or brokerage account.
  2. Pick a diversified, low-cost fund.
  3. Set up an automatic recurring investment on payday.
  4. Turn on dividend reinvestment.
  5. Raise the amount whenever your income goes up.
  6. Then ignore the daily market news.

The bottom line

For money you earn over time, dollar-cost averaging is one of the simplest, most effective investing habits there is. For money you already have, investing it all at once usually wins, but DCA is a fine choice if it helps you get invested and stay invested.
