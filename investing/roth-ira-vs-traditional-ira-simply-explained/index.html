Roth IRA vs. traditional IRA, simply explained

Both help you save for retirement with big tax advantages. The difference is when you pay the tax: now or later. Here's how to pick, with 2026 limits.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Traditional IRA: You may get a tax deduction now and pay tax when you withdraw in retirement.
  • Roth IRA: You pay tax now, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
  • For 2026, you can contribute up to $7,500 in total across your IRAs ($8,600 if you're 50 or older).

An IRA (individual retirement account) is an account you open yourself, separate from any workplace plan, that gives your investments special tax treatment. The big question is which type to choose. It mostly comes down to one thing: do you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement?

How the tax trade-off works

Imagine you put $7,500 into an IRA and it grows to $60,000 by retirement.

  • Traditional: You might save on taxes this year. In retirement, all $60,000 is taxed as income when you withdraw it.
  • Roth: You pay taxes on the $7,500 this year. In retirement, the full $60,000 is yours, tax-free.

If your tax rate in retirement is higher than today, the Roth wins. If it's lower, the traditional IRA usually wins. If it's the same, they come out about even.

When a Roth IRA usually makes sense

  • You're early in your career and in a lower tax bracket than you expect to be later.
  • You want tax-free income in retirement and flexibility to manage taxes then.
  • You value being able to withdraw your contributions in an emergency.
  • You'd like to avoid required minimum distributions.

When a traditional IRA usually makes sense

  • You're in your peak earning years and a high tax bracket now.
  • You expect a lower income in retirement.
  • Your income is too high to contribute to a Roth directly, though you may still be able to use a "backdoor Roth." Talk with a tax professional first.
  • You need the deduction to lower your taxable income this year.
Can't decide? Split it. Nobody knows future tax rates. Having some money in each type ("tax diversification") gives you flexibility to choose which account to withdraw from in retirement.

Common questions

Can I have both an IRA and a 401(k)?

Yes. You can contribute to both. If you or your spouse is covered by a workplace plan, your traditional IRA deduction may be limited based on income, but you can still contribute.

Can I have both a Roth and a traditional IRA?

Yes, but the $7,500 limit is shared between them. You could put $4,000 in one and $3,500 in the other.

What's the deadline?

You can generally make contributions for a tax year until that year's tax filing deadline, usually in mid-April of the following year.

What does "qualified" mean for Roth withdrawals?

Generally, you must be at least 59½ and have had a Roth IRA for at least five years for earnings to come out tax-free.

Do I need earned income?

Yes. You (or your spouse, if filing jointly) need earned income at least equal to what you contribute.

How to open one

  1. Choose a low-cost brokerage.
  2. Open a Roth or traditional IRA online. It takes about 15 minutes.
  3. Fund it with a transfer from your bank account, and set up automatic monthly contributions if you can.
  4. Invest the money. Many people forget this step and leave contributions sitting in cash. A broad index fund or target-date fund is a simple choice.

The bottom line

Roth means pay tax now and none later. Traditional means a possible tax break now and pay tax later. If you're unsure and eligible, a Roth is a great default for many younger savers. Most importantly, open one, contribute regularly and invest the money.

Limits shown are for 2026 and change annually. Tax situations vary, so consider consulting a tax professional. This article is for education only and isn't tax or investment advice.
