What is an expense ratio and why it matters

It's a fee you never see on a bill, and that's exactly why it's easy to ignore. Over a lifetime of investing, the difference between a cheap fund and an expensive one can reach tens of thousands of dollars.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20265 min read
The short version
  • An expense ratio is the yearly fee a fund charges, as a percentage of your investment.
  • It's taken automatically from the fund's returns, so you never see a bill.
  • Investing $500 a month for 30 years, the difference between a 0.05% fund and a 1% fund could be about $87,000.

When you buy a mutual fund or ETF, you pay the fund company to run it. That cost is the expense ratio. It's one of the few things in investing you can control completely, and one of the best predictors of how much of the market's return you'll actually keep.

How it works

An expense ratio of 0.10% means you pay $1 a year for every $1,000 invested. The fee isn't charged separately. It's quietly deducted from the fund's assets, so the returns you see are already net of fees.

Expense ratioYearly cost on $10,000Yearly cost on $100,000
0.03%$3$30
0.10%$10$100
0.50%$50$500
1.00%$100$1,000

Why small percentages become big money

Fees compound just like returns, but against you. Every dollar paid in fees is a dollar that never gets to grow. Here's someone investing $500 a month for 30 years, assuming the market returns 7% a year before fees:

Expense ratioEnding balance
0.05%~$562,000
0.40%~$528,000
1.00%~$474,000

Same deposits, same market. The only difference is the fee, and the gap between the cheapest and the most expensive is about $87,000.

What's a "good" expense ratio?

Fees have fallen dramatically. According to the Investment Company Institute, the average expense ratio investors paid for equity mutual funds dropped from 1.04% in 1996 to 0.40% in 2025. Index equity mutual funds averaged just 0.05%. A rough guide:

  • Broad stock or bond index funds and ETFs: under 0.10% is excellent; under 0.20% is good.
  • Target-date funds: under 0.30% is reasonable; index-based versions are often much cheaper.
  • Actively managed funds: often 0.50% to 1%+. Ask whether the manager is really likely to earn that back.
  • Specialty or niche ETFs: often 0.30% to 0.75%+.

Do more expensive funds perform better?

Usually, no. Research has repeatedly found that most actively managed funds trail their benchmark index over long periods, largely because of their higher costs. A low fee doesn't guarantee great performance, but a high fee is a guaranteed headwind.

Other costs to watch

The expense ratio isn't the only fee investors pay:

  • Sales loads: a commission of up to several percent when you buy or sell some mutual funds. Look for "no-load" funds.
  • Advisory fees: often around 1% a year of your assets, charged on top of fund expenses.
  • Account fees: annual maintenance or inactivity fees at some brokerages.
  • 401(k) administrative fees: check your plan's annual fee disclosure.

How to find a fund's expense ratio

  1. Search the fund's ticker symbol on your brokerage's site or the fund company's website.
  2. Look for "expense ratio" or "net expense ratio" on the summary page.
  3. For your 401(k), check the plan's investment lineup or fee disclosure document.

What to do today

  1. List every fund you own and its expense ratio.
  2. Flag anything above 0.50% and ask whether a cheaper option holds similar investments.
  3. In your 401(k), see if a low-cost index fund is available. Most plans have at least one.
  4. In taxable accounts, consider any capital gains taxes before switching funds.

The bottom line

You can't control the market, but you can control what you pay. Choosing low-cost funds is one of the simplest, most reliable ways to keep more of your returns, and over decades it can be worth as much as years of extra saving.

Examples are hypothetical and assume steady returns; actual results vary. This article is for education only and isn't investment advice.
