How to build a three-fund portfolio

Three low-cost index funds can give you a globally diversified portfolio that's simple to manage and hard to beat. Here's how to build one and choose your mix.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • A three-fund portfolio holds a total U.S. stock fund, a total international stock fund and a total bond fund.
  • Together they cover thousands of stocks and bonds worldwide, at very low cost.
  • Your main decision is the mix between stocks and bonds, which depends on your timeline and comfort with risk.

Investing can feel like it requires dozens of funds and constant tinkering. It doesn't. The three-fund portfolio, popularized by the Bogleheads community (fans of Vanguard founder John Bogle), shows that three broad index funds can give you nearly everything a well-diversified investor needs.

The three funds

FundWhat it holdsRole
1. Total U.S. stock marketThousands of U.S. companies, large and smallMain growth engine
2. Total international stockThousands of companies in developed and emerging marketsGlobal diversification
3. Total U.S. bond marketGovernment and high-quality corporate bondsStability and income; cushions stock drops

Most major fund companies offer all three as low-cost index mutual funds or ETFs, often for well under 0.10% a year.

Why it works

  • Maximum diversification: You own a slice of most of the world's public companies and the U.S. bond market.
  • Rock-bottom costs: Broad index funds are among the cheapest investments available.
  • Simplicity: Three funds are easy to understand, track and rebalance.
  • No guessing: You don't need to pick sectors, countries or stocks.

Step 1: Choose your stock and bond mix

Stocks grow more over long periods but swing more. Bonds grow less but steady the ride. A few sample mixes:

ProfileU.S. stocksInt'l stocksBonds
Aggressive (20+ years to go)60%30%10%
Moderate (10–20 years)45%25%30%
Conservative (near or in retirement)30%15%55%

One classic rule of thumb is to hold roughly your age, or your age minus 10 to 20, as a percentage in bonds. It's only a starting point. The right mix is one you can stick with during a market crash.

Gut check: If your portfolio dropped 30% in a year, which has happened before, would you hold steady or sell? If you'd sell, add more bonds. Ending up with a mix you'll abandon in a panic is worse than having a slightly lower expected return.

Step 2: Decide your international share

Common approaches range from about 20% to 40% of your stock money in international funds. Global market-weight is closer to the high end. Many investors land around 30%. International stocks add diversification because U.S. and foreign markets don't always move together.

Step 3: Place funds in the right accounts

If you have more than one account type, where you hold each fund can save taxes:

  • Bonds often fit best in tax-advantaged accounts like a 401(k) or traditional IRA, because their interest is taxed as ordinary income.
  • U.S. and international stock funds are relatively tax-efficient in taxable accounts. International funds held in a taxable account may also qualify you for the foreign tax credit.
  • If your 401(k) lacks one of the three, use the closest option there and fill the gap in your IRA or brokerage account. Think of all your accounts as one portfolio.

Step 4: Rebalance once a year

Over time, the investments that do well grow into a bigger share of your portfolio, so your mix drifts. Once a year (or when any fund is more than about 5 percentage points off target):

  1. Check your current percentages.
  2. Direct new contributions to whichever fund is below target. This avoids selling and taxes.
  3. If needed, sell some of the overweight fund and buy the underweight one, preferably inside tax-advantaged accounts.

Rebalancing forces you to buy low and sell high, without any guessing.

A simpler alternative: one fund

If even three funds feels like too much, a target-date fund or a balanced "all-in-one" fund holds a similar mix inside a single fund and rebalances automatically. You pay a bit more in fees, but there's nothing to manage. Just don't combine a target-date fund with other funds unless you understand how they overlap.

Common mistakes

  • Adding "just one more" fund. Extra funds usually overlap with what you already own and add complexity.
  • Changing your mix after the market moves. Decide your mix when you're calm, and stick to it.
  • Never rebalancing. Your risk level drifts without you noticing.
  • Getting too conservative too early. Long-term goals need growth to beat inflation.

The bottom line

Three low-cost index funds, a mix you can live with and a yearly rebalance: that's a complete investment plan. It's not exciting, and that's exactly why it works.
