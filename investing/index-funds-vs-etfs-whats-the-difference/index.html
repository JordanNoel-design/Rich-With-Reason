Index funds vs. ETFs: what's the difference?

They're often described as rivals, but an ETF can be an index fund, and many index funds come in both forms. Here's what actually separates them and how to choose.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • An index fund is a strategy: it tracks a market index instead of picking stocks.
  • An ETF is a wrapper: a fund that trades on the stock market like a share.
  • Many popular ETFs are index funds. The real choice is usually between an index mutual fund and an index ETF, and both can be excellent.

If you've started reading about investing, you've probably seen advice like "just buy index funds" next to "ETFs are the best way to start." It sounds like two competing options. They aren't, exactly. Once you see how the terms fit together, the choice gets much simpler.

What is an index fund?

An index fund owns all, or a representative sample, of the investments in a market index, such as the S&P 500 (500 large U.S. companies) or a "total market" index covering thousands of stocks. It doesn't try to beat the market. It tries to be the market.

Why that's powerful:

  • Low costs. There are no expensive analysts picking stocks. In 2025 the average index equity mutual fund cost investors just 0.05% a year, according to the Investment Company Institute.
  • Instant diversification. One fund can hold hundreds or thousands of companies.
  • Strong long-term results. Over long periods, most actively managed funds fail to beat their index after fees.

What is an ETF?

An exchange-traded fund is a fund that trades on a stock exchange throughout the day, just like an individual stock. Most ETFs track an index, so they're index funds too. Some ETFs are actively managed, and some track narrow or complex strategies.

The real comparison: index mutual fund vs. index ETF

Why ETFs tend to be more tax-efficient

When mutual fund investors cash out, the fund sometimes has to sell holdings, which can create capital gains distributions for everyone in the fund. ETFs usually handle redemptions in a way that avoids selling, so they distribute fewer taxable gains. This only matters in a regular taxable brokerage account. In a 401(k) or IRA, it doesn't matter.

Which one should you choose?

Choose an index ETF if…

  • You're investing in a regular taxable brokerage account.
  • You're starting small and want to buy with any amount.
  • You want flexibility to move your account to any brokerage.

Choose an index mutual fund if…

  • You want to automatically invest an exact dollar amount every payday.
  • You're investing inside a 401(k), where mutual funds are often the only option.
  • You like not seeing prices bounce around during the day. It removes the temptation to trade.
Honest answer: For a long-term investor, the difference between a low-cost S&P 500 index mutual fund and a low-cost S&P 500 index ETF is tiny. Picking a broad, cheap fund and investing consistently matters far more than which wrapper it comes in.

What to look for in any index fund or ETF

  1. Expense ratio: For broad U.S. stock index funds, look for well under 0.20%, ideally under 0.10%.
  2. What it tracks: Broad indexes (total market, S&P 500, total international, total bond) are best for most people.
  3. Size and trading volume: For ETFs, larger, heavily traded funds usually have tighter bid-ask spreads (the small gap between buying and selling prices).
  4. Tracking: A good fund stays very close to its index's return.

Watch out for these

  • Leveraged and inverse ETFs (often labeled 2x, 3x or "short"). They're built for short-term trading and can lose value over time even if the market goes nowhere.
  • Very narrow themes, such as a single trend or technology. They're more concentrated and often more expensive.
  • Overtrading. Being able to trade ETFs all day is a feature, not an invitation.

The bottom line

"Index fund" describes what a fund owns. "ETF" describes how you buy it. For most people, a broad, low-cost index fund, in either form, is one of the best investing tools available. Pick the one that fits your account, then focus on investing regularly.

Common questions

Is an ETF an index fund?

Many are. 'Index fund' describes the strategy of tracking a market index, while 'ETF' describes how the fund trades. Most popular ETFs are index funds, but some ETFs are actively managed.

Are ETFs better than index mutual funds?

In a taxable brokerage account, ETFs are usually a bit more tax-efficient and easier to buy with small amounts. In a 401(k) or IRA, a low-cost index mutual fund works just as well.

What is a good expense ratio for an index fund?

For a broad U.S. stock index fund or ETF, under 0.10% is excellent and under 0.20% is good.

Can you lose money in an index fund?

Yes. Index funds rise and fall with the market, so they can lose value, especially in the short term. Over long periods, broad stock indexes have historically grown.
