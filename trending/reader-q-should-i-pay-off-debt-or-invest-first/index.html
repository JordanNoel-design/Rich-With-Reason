Reader Q: Should I pay off debt or invest first?

It's one of the most common money questions we get. The answer depends mostly on one number, your interest rate, plus a few rules that come first. Here's the framework.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Always get your employer 401(k) match first. It's an instant return you can't get anywhere else.
  • Pay off high-interest debt (roughly 8% and up, like credit cards) before investing beyond the match.
  • For low-interest debt (roughly under 5%), investing usually makes more sense. In between, splitting your money is reasonable.

"I have about $500 extra each month. I've got $6,000 on a credit card, a car loan at 6% and student loans at 4.5%. Should I pay those off first or start investing?"

Great question, and a common one. There's no single answer, but there is a clear framework. It comes down to comparing the guaranteed return of paying off debt with the expected (but not guaranteed) return of investing.

Paying off debt is a guaranteed return

Every dollar you put toward a credit card charging 24% is like earning 24% risk-free, because that's interest you'll never pay. No investment reliably offers that. Stocks have historically averaged somewhere around 7% to 10% a year over long periods, but with big ups and downs and no guarantees.

The priority order

  1. Build a starter emergency fund ($1,000 to $2,000). Without it, the next surprise goes right back on the credit card.
  2. Get your full employer 401(k) match. A 50% or 100% match on your contributions is an immediate return that beats paying off almost any debt.
  3. Pay off high-interest debt: credit cards, payday loans and most personal loans above roughly 8%.
  4. Build your full emergency fund of 3 to 6 months of essential expenses.
  5. Medium-interest debt (about 5% to 8%): split extra money between paying it down and investing, or pick based on your comfort level.
  6. Low-interest debt (under about 5%): pay the minimum and invest the rest, especially in tax-advantaged accounts.

Applying it to our reader

DebtRatePriority
Credit card ($6,000)~24%Pay off aggressively
Car loan6%Gray zone: steady payments, maybe a little extra
Student loans4.5%Minimum payments; invest instead

Our suggested plan:

  • Make sure there's a starter emergency fund and that the full 401(k) match is captured.
  • Send the full $500 extra to the credit card. At 24%, it would be paid off in a little over a year, or faster with a 0% balance transfer.
  • Once the card is gone, send most of that $500 into a Roth IRA or extra 401(k) contributions, while paying the minimums on the car and student loans.

Factors that can change the answer

  • Taxes: Investing through a 401(k), IRA or HSA adds tax benefits. On the other side, student loan interest may be partly tax-deductible.
  • Variable vs. fixed rates: Debt with a rate that can rise, like credit cards and some private loans, deserves extra priority.
  • Your timeline: Investing is for money you won't need for at least five years.
  • Peace of mind: Some people sleep better debt-free even when the math slightly favors investing. That's a valid choice, especially for medium-rate debt.
  • Federal student loans: They may offer income-driven repayment or forgiveness options worth considering before you pay them off early.
  • Today's rates: With top savings accounts paying around 4% and Treasury yields elevated in late 2026, paying off debt below about 4% to 5% is less compelling than it would be if rates were near zero.
Quick rule of thumb: If the interest rate on a debt is higher than the return you could reasonably expect from investing after taxes, pay the debt. If it's lower, invest. If it's close, do some of both.

Don't forget the time factor

Investing early is powerful because of compounding. A 25-year-old who waits five years to start investing may give up a large amount of future growth. That's why getting the employer match, and at least starting small, matters even while you pay down debt.

The bottom line

Get the match, kill high-interest debt, build your safety net, and then invest while paying off low-rate debt on schedule. It's not all-or-nothing. Most people do best with a clear order of priorities and automatic payments for each one.
