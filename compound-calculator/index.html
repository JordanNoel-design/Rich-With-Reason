What is compounding?

Compounding is what happens when your money earns a return, and then that return starts earning a return too. Instead of growing by the same amount every year, your balance grows on a bigger and bigger base. Given enough time, growth on top of growth does most of the heavy lifting.

A simple example

Say you invest $1,000 and it earns 7% a year. After one year you have $1,070. In year two you earn 7% on $1,070, not just on your original $1,000, so you end with about $1,145. That extra $4.90 looks tiny, but it keeps snowballing. Leave the money alone for 30 years and that single $1,000 grows to about $7,600, with nothing added.

Why monthly investing is so powerful

Most people don't invest one lump sum. They add a little every month. That's what the calculator above shows. Here's $300 a month at a 7% average annual return:

Time matters more than you think

Compare two savers, both earning 7% a year:

  • Saver A invests $200 a month from age 25 to 35, then stops completely. Total put in: $24,000.
  • Saver B waits, then invests $200 a month from age 35 to 65. Total put in: $72,000.

At 65, Saver A has about $281,000 and Saver B has about $244,000. Saver A put in a third as much money and still ends up ahead, because those early dollars had 30 extra years to compound.

The takeaway: you don't need a lot of money to benefit from compounding. You need time and consistency. Starting small today usually beats starting big later.

The Rule of 72

A quick way to estimate how long it takes money to double: divide 72 by your annual return. At 7%, money doubles roughly every 10 years (72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3). At 4%, it takes about 18 years. At 10%, about 7.

The three levers you control

  1. How much you add. Raising your monthly contribution, even by $25, adds up over decades.
  2. How long you stay invested. The biggest gains come at the end, so staying the course through ups and downs matters.
  3. What you keep. High fees and taxes eat into your return every single year. Low-cost index funds and tax-advantaged accounts like a 401(k), IRA or HSA let more of your money compound.

Common questions

What is compound interest in simple terms?

It's interest on your interest. Each period, your return is added to your balance, so the next return is calculated on a bigger amount. Over long periods that makes your money grow faster and faster.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is paid only on your original amount. Compound interest is paid on your original amount plus the interest you've already earned. $1,000 at 7% simple interest for 30 years becomes $3,100; compounded once a year, it becomes about $7,600.

What return should I use in a compound interest calculator?

The U.S. stock market has historically averaged around 10% a year before inflation, with big swings. Many people plan with 5% to 7% to stay conservative and to roughly account for inflation. For a savings account, use its current APY.

How often is interest compounded?

Savings accounts usually compound daily or monthly. With investments like index funds, compounding happens when gains and dividends stay invested and grow. This calculator assumes monthly compounding.
