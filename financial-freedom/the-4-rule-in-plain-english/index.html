The 4% rule in plain English

It's the most famous number in retirement planning, and the most misunderstood. Here's what the 4% rule actually says, where it came from and how experts see it today.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • The 4% rule says you can withdraw about 4% of your savings in your first year of retirement, raise that dollar amount with inflation each year, and very likely not run out of money for 30 years.
  • Its creator, Bill Bengen, now puts the number at 4.7%. Morningstar's 2026 estimate is 3.9%.
  • Treat it as a starting point for planning, not a guarantee.

"How much can I spend in retirement without running out of money?" It's one of the hardest questions in personal finance. The 4% rule is the most popular short answer. To use it well, you need to know what's behind it.

Where the 4% rule came from

In 1994, financial planner William Bengen studied historical U.S. stock and bond returns going back decades. He asked a simple question: what's the highest starting withdrawal rate that would have survived every 30-year retirement in history, including the worst ones, like retiring just before the high inflation of the late 1960s and 1970s?

His answer was about 4%. A few years later, the well-known "Trinity study" reached similar conclusions. The rule stuck.

How it actually works

People often get this part wrong. The rule isn't "withdraw 4% of whatever your balance is each year." It works like this:

  1. In year one, withdraw 4% of your starting portfolio.
  2. In each later year, take the same dollar amount as the year before, adjusted for inflation, no matter what the market did.
Example: You retire with $1,000,000. In year one you withdraw $40,000. If inflation runs 3% that year, year two's withdrawal is $41,200. Year three adjusts again from there.

Flip it around to find your target

If 4% of your savings is what you can spend, then you need about 25 times your yearly spending to retire. Planning to spend $50,000 a year from your portfolio? Your target is about $1.25 million. That's where the popular "×25" rule comes from.

So is it 4%, 4.7% or 3.9%?

The rule has been revisited many times, and today's experts land on slightly different numbers:

SourceStarting rateWhy it differs
Original rule (1994)4.0%Historical worst case, simple stock and bond mix
Bill Bengen (2025 update)4.7%Adds more asset classes for broader diversification
Morningstar (2026 retirees)3.9%Forward-looking estimates of returns and inflation, 90% success rate

These numbers aren't really in conflict. They answer slightly different questions. Bengen's figure comes from history with a more diversified portfolio. Morningstar's comes from projecting future returns. Both say you'll probably be safe starting somewhere around 4%.

What the rule doesn't account for

  • Retirements longer than 30 years. If you retire at 45, your money may need to last 45 years or more. Early retirees often plan around 3.25% to 3.5%.
  • Taxes and fees. The 4% is a pre-tax withdrawal. High investment fees reduce what you can safely take.
  • Your actual portfolio. The research assumes a diversified mix of stocks and bonds. A portfolio that's all cash or all one stock behaves very differently.
  • Other income. Social Security, pensions or part-time work reduce how much you need from your portfolio.
  • Your flexibility. The rule assumes you never cut spending, even in a crash. Real people usually do adjust.

The case for being flexible

The fixed-dollar approach is deliberately cautious. It was designed to survive the worst periods in history. In most periods, retirees following it ended up with more money than they started with. Bengen himself has noted that the average safe withdrawal rate across history was much higher than his worst-case figure.

That's why many planners recommend guardrails: start near 4%, trim spending a little after a bad market year and give yourself a raise after strong years. Morningstar's research finds that retirees who are willing to adjust spending can start meaningfully higher than 3.9%.

How to use the 4% rule today

  1. Use it for planning. Multiply your expected yearly spending (minus Social Security or pension income) by 25 to set a savings target.
  2. Adjust for your timeline. Retiring early? Use 28 to 30 times your spending to add a margin of safety.
  3. Keep a cash buffer. One to two years of spending in cash or short-term bonds can keep you from selling stocks in a downturn.
  4. Revisit every year. Check your spending, your portfolio and the markets, and adjust.
  5. Get personalized help as you get close to retirement. Taxes, Social Security timing and account withdrawals can change the math a lot.

The bottom line

The 4% rule is a useful, research-backed rule of thumb. It turns "how much do I need?" into a number you can work toward. Just remember it describes a cautious starting point, not a promise. Pair it with flexibility and an annual check-up, and it's a solid foundation for a retirement plan.

Common questions

How does the 4% rule work?

Withdraw 4% of your savings in your first year of retirement, then take the same dollar amount each year after, adjusted for inflation. Historically, that lasted at least 30 years in nearly every period studied.

Is the 4% rule still valid in 2026?

It's still a reasonable starting point. Morningstar's estimate for 2026 retirees is 3.9%, while the rule's creator, Bill Bengen, now suggests 4.7% for a more diversified portfolio.

How much do I need to retire using the 4% rule?

Multiply the yearly spending your portfolio must cover by 25. To withdraw $50,000 a year, you'd need about $1.25 million.

Does the 4% rule work for early retirement?

It was built for 30-year retirements. If you retire in your 40s, many planners suggest a lower rate of about 3.25% to 3.5%, or staying flexible with spending.
