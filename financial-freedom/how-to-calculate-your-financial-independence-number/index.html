Financial Freedom

How to calculate your financial independence number

Your FI number is the amount of invested money that could cover your living costs for life. Here's how to work out yours in about 15 minutes.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • FI number = (yearly spending − other reliable income) × 25.
  • Use 28 to 30 instead of 25 if you plan to retire early or want extra safety.
  • Recalculate once a year. Your number changes as your life does.

"Financial independence" can feel vague and far away. Your FI number makes it concrete: it's the size of the investment portfolio that could pay your bills without a paycheck. Once you know it, every savings decision has a clear target.

Step 1: Find your real yearly spending

This is the most important input, and the one most people guess at. Don't estimate. Look it up:

  • Pull 3 to 12 months of bank and credit card statements.
  • Add up everything you spent, including irregular costs like car repairs, gifts, travel and annual subscriptions.
  • Multiply your monthly average by 12.

Then adjust for retirement. You'll stop saving for retirement and stop paying payroll taxes, and you might pay off your mortgage. But health insurance before Medicare, travel and home maintenance might go up.

Step 2: Subtract reliable income

Any income you'll have without working reduces how much your portfolio has to cover:

  • Social Security. You can see your estimate at ssa.gov with a free my Social Security account.
  • A pension.
  • Rental income, after expenses.
  • Part-time work you actually plan to do.

If you're retiring well before Social Security age, you may need to plan in two phases: a "bridge" period before benefits start, and the years after.

Step 3: Multiply by your safety factor

The classic multiplier is 25. It comes from the 4% rule, which suggests a diversified portfolio can support yearly withdrawals of about 4% of its starting value, adjusted for inflation, for about 30 years.

Your situationMultiplierWithdrawal rate
Traditional retirement (~30 years)25×4.0%
Want a little extra cushion28×~3.6%
Early retirement (40+ years)30×~3.3%

A worked example

Dana spends $48,000 a year. At full retirement age she expects about $18,000 a year from Social Security.

  • Spending her portfolio must cover: $48,000 − $18,000 = $30,000
  • FI number at 25×: $30,000 × 25 = $750,000
  • With extra cushion at 28×: $840,000

If Dana wants to stop working before Social Security starts, she'll also need enough set aside to cover the full $48,000 during those bridge years.

The hidden lever: Every $100 a month you permanently cut from your spending lowers your FI number by about $30,000 ($1,200 a year × 25). Cutting spending works twice: it shrinks your target and frees more money to invest.

Step 4: Find your timeline

Now compare your FI number with what you have and what you save each year. A rough rule: the higher your savings rate (the share of take-home pay you save and invest), the faster you get there. Starting from zero and assuming about 5% growth after inflation:

Savings rateYears to FI (approx.)
10%52
20%37
30%28
50%17
70%9

Online FIRE calculators can model your exact numbers, including what you've already saved.

Also calculate your "Coast FI" number

Coast FI is the amount you'd need invested today so that growth alone gets you to your FI number by your target age, even if you never save another dollar. The formula is:

Coast FI = FI number ÷ (1 + growth rate)years until retirement
Example: a $1,000,000 goal in 35 years at 5% growth after inflation is $1,000,000 ÷ 1.0535 ≈ $181,000 today.

Hitting Coast FI is a huge milestone. It means you could take a lower-stress job or work part-time and still be on track for retirement.

Common mistakes

  • Underestimating spending. Forgetting irregular costs is the number-one error. Add a 10% buffer if you're unsure.
  • Forgetting health care. If you retire before 65, budget for marketplace insurance and out-of-pocket costs.
  • Ignoring taxes. Withdrawals from traditional 401(k)s and IRAs are taxed as income. Your spending number should include those taxes.
  • Counting your home. Your house only counts toward FI if you plan to sell it or rent it out. Otherwise, leave it out.

Do it now: your 15-minute worksheet

  1. Yearly spending: $______
  2. Minus reliable yearly income in retirement: $______
  3. = Amount your portfolio must cover: $______
  4. × 25 (or 28 to 30 for early retirement) = Your FI number: $______
  5. Current invested assets: $______ → percent of the way there: ______%

The bottom line

Your FI number turns a fuzzy dream into a measurable goal. Calculate it, write it down and check it once a year. Watching that "percent of the way there" number climb is one of the most motivating things in personal finance.

Common questions

What is an FI number?

The amount of invested money that could cover your living costs for life. A common formula is your yearly spending minus other reliable income, such as Social Security, times 25.

Should I include my house in my FI number?

Usually not. Your home only counts toward financial independence if you plan to sell it or rent it out. Otherwise it doesn't produce income to live on.

What multiplier should I use for early retirement?

Use 28 to 30 times your yearly spending instead of 25 if you plan to retire early or want extra safety, because your money may need to last 40 years or more.

Calculations are simplified and assume steady growth; real returns vary. This article is for education only and isn't financial advice.
