Financial Freedom

Why your savings rate matters more than returns

You can't control what the market does. You can control how much you save. For most people, especially early on, that's the number that decides when they're free.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Your savings rate is the share of your take-home pay that you save and invest.
  • Early in your journey, what you put in matters far more than what your investments earn.
  • A higher savings rate helps twice: you invest more and you need less to live on.

People spend hours hunting for the "best" investment, trying to squeeze an extra 1% or 2% of return out of the market. Meanwhile the most powerful number in their financial life gets almost no attention: how much they actually save. Here's why your savings rate deserves the spotlight.

What is a savings rate?

Savings rate = (money saved and invested) ÷ (take-home pay)
Example: You take home $4,000 a month and put $600 into your 401(k), IRA and savings. Your savings rate is 15%. If your employer adds a match, many people count that too.

Some people calculate it from gross (pre-tax) income. Either way works. Just be consistent so you can track it over time.

Reason 1: Early on, contributions beat growth

When your balance is small, even great returns don't add up to much in dollars. A 10% return on $5,000 is $500. Adding $500 a month is $6,000 a year. For the first several years, your deposits drive almost all of your progress. Returns only take over once your balance gets large.

Reason 2: Saving more beats earning more, by a lot

Here's a 20-year example for someone earning $60,000 a year:

ScenarioSaves each yearReturnAfter 20 years
A: Chases returns$9,000 (15%)8%~$412,000
B: Saves more$15,000 (25%)6%~$552,000

Person B earned a lower return every single year and still ended up with about $140,000 more. And B's result is far more certain. Nobody can guarantee 8% a year, but you can set your savings rate yourself.

Reason 3: It works on both sides of the equation

This is the part most people miss. When you save more, you're also proving you can live on less. That lowers the amount you need to retire. A common target is about 25 times your yearly spending, so:

  • Living on $60,000 a year → you need about $1.5 million.
  • Living on $50,000 a year → you need about $1.25 million.

Raising your savings rate speeds you toward the finish line and moves the finish line closer to you.

The savings rate timeline

Starting from zero, assuming about 5% growth after inflation and a target of 25 times your spending, here's roughly how long it takes to reach financial independence:

Savings rateYears to FI
10%52
20%37
30%28
40%22
50%17
60%13
70%9

Notice that this table barely mentions investment returns. Your savings rate alone sets the rough timeline.

Returns still matter, so get the basics right

None of this means investing doesn't matter. Over decades, returns do most of the heavy lifting. But you don't need to be clever to get good returns. You need to avoid the things that drag them down:

  • Keep fees low. A 1% yearly fee can take a big bite out of a lifetime of returns. Broad index funds often cost under 0.10%.
  • Stay diversified. Own the whole market instead of a handful of stocks.
  • Don't panic-sell. Selling after a crash locks in losses and misses the recovery.
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts. Your 401(k), IRA and HSA let more of your money stay invested.

Do these four things and your returns will mostly take care of themselves. That leaves your energy for the lever you actually control.

How to raise your savings rate without misery

  1. Pay yourself first. Automate transfers on payday so saving happens before spending.
  2. Save your raises. Each time your pay goes up, send at least half of the increase to savings. You won't miss money you never got used to.
  3. Go after the big three. Housing, transportation and food usually make up most of a budget. One win here beats dozens of small cuts.
  4. Grab the full employer match. It's an instant, risk-free boost to your savings rate.
  5. Raise it 1% at a time. Many 401(k) plans can do this for you automatically every year.
  6. Add income. A side hustle invested in full can add 5 to 10 points to your rate.

The bottom line

You can't control the market. You can control your savings rate, and for most people it's the number that decides how soon they're free. Calculate yours today, write it down and try to nudge it up a little every few months.

Common questions

What is a good savings rate?

Saving 15% to 20% of your income is a solid goal for most people. Higher rates, like 30% to 50%, are how people reach financial independence much earlier.

How do you calculate your savings rate?

Divide the money you save and invest each month by your take-home pay. If you take home $4,000 and save $600, your savings rate is 15%.

How many years to financial independence at a 50% savings rate?

Roughly 17 years starting from zero, assuming about 5% growth after inflation. At a 20% savings rate, it's about 37 years.
