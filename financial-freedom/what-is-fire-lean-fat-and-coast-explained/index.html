What is FIRE? Lean, Fat and Coast explained

FIRE stands for Financial Independence, Retire Early. It isn't one plan but a family of them. Here's how each version works and who it fits.

By the Rich With Reason teamNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • FIRE means saving and investing aggressively until your investments can cover your living costs, so work becomes optional.
  • The most common target is about 25 times your yearly spending.
  • Lean, Fat, Coast and Barista FIRE are different trade-offs between how much you spend and how soon you're free.

The FIRE movement started with a simple idea that sounds radical: if you save a big share of your income and invest it, you can reach the point where your investments pay your bills decades before traditional retirement age. Some people use that freedom to stop working completely. Many more use it to work on their own terms.

The core math

Most FIRE plans are built on the "4% rule," a rule of thumb from retirement research. It suggests that a diversified portfolio can support yearly withdrawals of about 4% of its starting value, adjusted for inflation, for around 30 years. Flip that around and you get the classic target:

FI number = your yearly spending × 25
Spend $40,000 a year? Your target is about $1,000,000. Spend $30,000? About $750,000.

That's why FIRE is as much about spending as earning. Every dollar you cut from your yearly costs lowers your target by $25 and frees up another dollar to invest.

The four main flavors

Lean FIRE

What it is: Reaching independence on a minimalist budget, often well under the typical household's spending.

Pros: A smaller target means you can get there fastest.

Cons: Thin margin for surprises like medical bills, a new roof or inflation. Lean FIRE works best for people who genuinely enjoy a simple life, not for those white-knuckling it.

Fat FIRE

What it is: Independence with a comfortable or generous budget, often $100,000 a year or more.

Pros: Plenty of cushion, and room for travel, hobbies and helping family.

Cons: A target of $2.5 million or more usually takes a high income, a long runway or both.

Coast FIRE

What it is: You've saved enough that, without adding another dollar, your investments should grow to your full retirement number by a traditional retirement age. You still work to pay today's bills, but you no longer need to save for retirement.

Example: If you want $1 million (in today's dollars) at 65 and expect about 5% growth after inflation, you'd need roughly $181,000 invested at age 30 to "coast" there.

Pros: Huge pressure relief. You can take a lower-paying job you love, work part-time or take a career break.

Barista FIRE

What it is: Your investments cover part of your costs, and part-time or flexible work covers the rest. The name comes from the idea of a part-time job that also offers health insurance.

Pros: You leave full-time work years earlier than with full FIRE, and the work you keep can add structure and social connection.

What makes FIRE work

  1. A high savings rate. This is the single biggest lever. Someone saving 50% of their take-home pay can reach independence in roughly 17 years, compared with about 37 years at a 20% savings rate (assuming 5% growth after inflation).
  2. Low-cost, diversified investing. Most FIRE followers use broad index funds with tiny fees.
  3. Using tax-advantaged accounts. 401(k)s, IRAs and HSAs cut your tax bill while you build wealth.
  4. Growing your income. Raises, job changes and side income speed things up more than extreme frugality alone.

The challenges nobody should skip

  • Health insurance before 65. Without an employer plan, you'll likely use the ACA marketplace. Budget for it carefully.
  • Getting to your retirement money early. Withdrawals from 401(k)s and IRAs before 59½ usually come with a 10% penalty, though there are legal workarounds, such as withdrawing Roth contributions (not earnings) or using certain IRS-approved payment plans. Early retirees often keep a taxable brokerage account as a "bridge."
  • Longer time horizons. A 40- or 50-year retirement is longer than the 30 years the 4% rule was built for. Many early retirees plan with a lower withdrawal rate, around 3.25% to 3.5%, or stay flexible with their spending.
  • The human side. Some people find that the hardest part of early retirement is purpose, not money. Plan what you're retiring to.

How to start, even if early retirement isn't your goal

  1. Track your spending for one month to find your real yearly number.
  2. Multiply by 25 to see your rough FI number.
  3. Calculate your savings rate: what you save and invest divided by your take-home pay.
  4. Raise it by a few points, and then automate it.

The bottom line

You don't have to retire at 40 to benefit from FIRE thinking. Knowing your number, spending on purpose and investing the gap gives you options, whether that's retiring early, switching careers or simply sleeping better.

Examples are simplified and assume steady returns. Real markets go up and down, and your situation may differ. This article is for education only and isn't financial advice.
