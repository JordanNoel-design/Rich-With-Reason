Term vs. whole life insurance: which do you need?

One is simple, temporary and cheap. The other is permanent, complex and expensive. Here's how term and whole life insurance really compare, and why most families start with term.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Term life covers you for a set period, like 20 or 30 years. It's pure protection and very affordable.
  • Whole life covers you for life and builds cash value, but costs many times more for the same death benefit.
  • Most people who need life insurance to protect dependents are well served by term.

Life insurance exists for one main reason: to replace your income or cover your obligations if you die while people depend on you. Both term and whole life do that. The difference is in how long they last, what else they do and how much they cost.

Term life insurance

You choose a coverage amount and a term, typically 10, 20 or 30 years. If you die during the term, your beneficiaries get the death benefit, usually tax-free. If you outlive the term, the policy ends and nothing is paid out.

Cost: Very low for young, healthy people. A healthy 30-year-old non-smoker can often get a 20-year, $500,000 policy for roughly $20 to $30 a month in 2026. Premiums stay the same for the whole term.

Whole life insurance

Whole life is a type of permanent insurance. It covers you for your entire life as long as you pay premiums, and it includes a cash value account that grows slowly at a guaranteed rate. You can borrow against the cash value or surrender the policy for it.

Cost: High. The same $500,000 of coverage for that 30-year-old could cost several hundred dollars a month, often 10 times as much or more than term.

"Buy term and invest the difference"

This is the classic argument for term. Because term costs so much less, you can buy it and invest the money you save in a 401(k), IRA or index funds. For most people, those investments are likely to grow more over time than whole life cash value, and you keep control of the money.

Example: If term costs $25 a month and whole life costs $350, the difference is $325 a month. Invested for 20 years at a 6% average return, that's roughly $150,000, and you still had $500,000 of protection the whole time.

This only works if you actually invest the difference. If you'd spend it, whole life's forced savings could look more appealing, though it's an expensive way to save.

When whole life (or other permanent insurance) can make sense

  • Lifelong dependents, such as a child with special needs who will need support after you're gone.
  • Estate planning for high-net-worth families, for example to provide money to pay estate taxes or to equalize an inheritance.
  • Business needs, like funding a buy-sell agreement.
  • You've already maxed out your 401(k), IRA and HSA and want another tax-advantaged option. Get unbiased advice first.

Questions to ask before buying whole life

  • How much of my first few years of premiums goes to commissions and fees?
  • What's the guaranteed cash value after 5, 10 and 20 years, and what's the surrender charge if I cancel?
  • What happens if I can't afford the premiums later?
  • How do policy loans affect the death benefit?

Whole life is often sold by agents who earn large commissions on it. That doesn't make it bad, but it's a reason to get a second opinion, ideally from a fee-only financial planner who doesn't sell insurance.

How to choose a term length

Match your term to how long people will depend on your income:

  • Until your youngest child finishes school or college.
  • Until your mortgage is paid off.
  • Until you expect to have enough saved that your family wouldn't need the payout.

Some people "ladder" policies, for example a 30-year policy plus a 20-year policy, so coverage steps down as their needs shrink.

Tips for buying term

  1. Buy while you're young and healthy. Rates are based on age and health when you apply.
  2. Compare several insurers. Prices for the same coverage vary, and an independent broker can shop many companies at once.
  3. Check the insurer's financial strength rating.
  4. Look for a conversion option, which lets you convert to permanent coverage later without a new medical exam.
  5. Don't rely only on work coverage. Employer life insurance is often only 1 to 2 times your salary and usually ends if you leave the job.

The bottom line

For most families, term life insurance provides the protection they need at a price they can afford, leaving more money to invest. Whole life has a place for specific needs, but it's a complex, expensive product, so understand exactly what you're buying.

Common questions

Is term or whole life insurance better?

For most families who need to replace income while kids are young or a mortgage is unpaid, term is better. It's far cheaper for the same coverage. Whole life fits specific needs like lifelong dependents or estate planning.

How much does term life insurance cost?

A healthy 30-year-old non-smoker can often get a 20-year, $500,000 policy for roughly $20 to $30 a month in 2026. Rates depend on age, health and the insurer.

What happens when term life insurance ends?

Coverage stops and nothing is paid out. Many policies let you convert to permanent coverage without a new medical exam, which is worth looking for when you buy.

Rates are rough 2026 estimates for healthy non-smokers and vary by insurer, age, health and state. This article is for education only and isn't insurance or financial advice.
