Disability insurance: the coverage most people skip

Your ability to earn an income may be your biggest financial asset. Disability insurance protects it, yet many workers have little or none. Here's how it works and how much you need.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Disability insurance replaces part of your income if illness or injury keeps you from working.
  • The Social Security Administration estimates that more than 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before reaching retirement age.
  • Look for coverage that replaces about 60% to 70% of your income, ideally with an "own-occupation" definition.

People insure their homes, cars and phones, but often not the thing that pays for all of them: their paycheck. If you're 30 and earn $60,000 a year, you could earn more than $2 million over your career. Disability insurance protects that future income.

Why disability coverage matters

Most disabilities aren't caused by dramatic accidents. Illnesses like cancer, heart disease, back problems and mental health conditions are among the leading causes. Many people assume government benefits will cover them, but Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) has strict qualifying rules, often involves a long approval process and typically replaces only a modest portion of income.

Short-term vs. long-term disability

Short-term disabilityLong-term disability
StartsAfter a few days to two weeksAfter a waiting period, often 90 days
LastsA few months, often up to 6Years, sometimes until age 65 or 67
Typical income replacementOften 60% to 70%Often around 60%
Best forRecovery from surgery, injury or childbirthSerious, long-lasting illness or injury

Long-term disability coverage is the more important of the two. A few months without income is painful, but your emergency fund may cover it. Years without income is a financial catastrophe.

Check what you have through work

Many employers offer group disability insurance. Find out:

  • How much income it replaces, and whether there's a monthly cap. A 60% benefit capped at $5,000 a month may cover far less than 60% if you earn more.
  • Whether benefits are taxable. If your employer pays the premium, benefits are usually taxable. If you pay with after-tax dollars, benefits are usually tax-free.
  • How "disability" is defined. More on that below.
  • Whether you can take it with you. Group coverage usually ends when you leave the job.

Key features of a good policy

Definition of disability

  • Own-occupation: pays if you can't do your specific job, even if you could do another one. This is the strongest protection and especially valuable for specialized professionals.
  • Any-occupation: pays only if you can't do any job you're reasonably suited for. Easier to qualify for coverage, harder to collect.

Elimination (waiting) period

The time between becoming disabled and receiving benefits. A 90-day wait is common and keeps premiums lower, as long as your emergency fund can bridge the gap.

Benefit period

How long benefits last. To age 65 or 67 offers the most protection.

Useful add-ons ("riders")

  • Non-cancelable / guaranteed renewable: The insurer can't cancel your policy or raise your premium as long as you pay.
  • Residual or partial disability: pays a partial benefit if you can only work reduced hours.
  • Cost-of-living adjustment: increases benefits with inflation during a long claim.
  • Future increase option: lets you buy more coverage as your income grows, without new medical underwriting.

What does it cost?

Individual long-term disability insurance commonly costs 1% to 3% of your annual income. The price depends on your age, health, job, benefit amount, waiting period and features. Riskier jobs and stronger definitions cost more.

Example: On a $60,000 salary, 2% is $1,200 a year, or $100 a month. In return, you could protect $3,000 or more a month of income for decades if something happens.

How to get covered

  1. Enroll in your employer's plan if it's offered. It's usually the cheapest coverage available.
  2. Calculate your gap. Compare your take-home pay with what your work policy would actually pay.
  3. Consider an individual policy to fill the gap or provide coverage you can keep if you change jobs.
  4. Self-employed? You have no employer coverage, so an individual policy is especially important.
  5. Work with an independent broker who can compare several insurers.

The bottom line

Disability is more common than most people expect, and the financial impact can be devastating. Check your work coverage today, find out what it actually pays, and fill any gap. It's one of the most important and most overlooked parts of a solid financial plan.

Common questions

Do I need disability insurance?

If you rely on your paycheck, likely yes. The Social Security Administration estimates more than 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before retirement age.

How much does disability insurance cost?

Individual long-term disability insurance commonly costs about 1% to 3% of your annual income, depending on your age, health, job and the policy's features.

What is own-occupation disability insurance?

A policy that pays if you can't do your specific job, even if you could do a different one. It's the strongest type of protection and costs more.

Is employer disability insurance enough?

Often not. Group plans usually replace around 60% of pay up to a monthly cap, may be taxable and usually end when you leave the job.

Policy features and costs vary by insurer and state. This article is for education only and isn't insurance or financial advice.
