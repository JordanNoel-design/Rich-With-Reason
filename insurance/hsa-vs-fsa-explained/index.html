HSA vs. FSA explained

Both let you pay for health costs with pre-tax money. But one is yours forever and can be invested, while the other is mostly use-it-or-lose-it. Here's how they compare, with 2026 limits.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • HSA (Health Savings Account): requires an HSA-eligible health plan, rolls over forever, can be invested and stays with you if you change jobs.
  • FSA (Flexible Spending Account): offered through your employer and mostly use-it-or-lose-it each year.
  • 2026 limits: HSA $4,400 (self) or $8,750 (family); health FSA $3,400.

Health costs are a big part of most budgets. HSAs and FSAs let you pay for many of them with money that's never taxed, which can save you hundreds or even thousands of dollars a year. But they work very differently.

The HSA: a triple tax advantage

An HSA is the only account in the tax code with three tax breaks:

  1. Contributions are tax-deductible, or pre-tax if made through payroll.
  2. Growth is tax-free.
  3. Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

After age 65, you can withdraw for any reason without penalty. Non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income, like a traditional IRA. That's why many people treat an HSA as a stealth retirement account: they pay current medical costs out of pocket, invest the HSA and save receipts so they can reimburse themselves tax-free years later.

Who qualifies for an HSA in 2026?

  • You must be enrolled in an HSA-eligible plan. Traditionally that's a high-deductible health plan, with a minimum deductible of $1,700 for self-only or $3,400 for family coverage in 2026.
  • New for 2026: Under the 2025 tax law, bronze and catastrophic plans on the ACA marketplace now count as HSA-eligible. Certain direct primary care arrangements no longer disqualify you either.
  • You can't be enrolled in Medicare or claimed as someone's dependent, and generally can't have other non-qualifying health coverage, including a general-purpose FSA.

The FSA: simple, but use it or lose it

An FSA lets you set aside pre-tax money from your paycheck for medical costs like copays, prescriptions, dental work and glasses. Two big perks: you can use your whole annual election on January 1, even before you've contributed it, and you don't need a high-deductible plan.

The catch is the use-it-or-lose-it rule. Employers can offer either a carryover (up to $680 into 2027) or a grace period of up to two and a half months, but not both, or neither.

FSA tip: Estimate conservatively. Add up predictable costs like prescriptions, regular copays, planned dental work and glasses, and contribute about that much. Don't guess high.

What about a "limited-purpose" FSA?

If you have an HSA, some employers offer a limited-purpose FSA for dental and vision costs only. You can have both, which lets you save HSA money for later while covering dental and vision expenses with the FSA.

What can you spend them on?

Qualified medical expenses include deductibles, copays, prescriptions, dental and vision care, many over-the-counter medicines, menstrual care products, and some equipment and services. Check IRS Publication 502 or your plan administrator for the full list.

Which should you choose?

An HSA is often better if…

  • You're generally healthy and have an HSA-eligible plan.
  • You can afford to cover the deductible if needed.
  • You want a long-term, investable, tax-free account.

An FSA may be better if…

  • Your employer doesn't offer an HSA-eligible plan.
  • You have predictable medical costs each year.
  • You prefer a lower-deductible health plan.

Don't forget the dependent care FSA

This is a separate account for childcare costs, not medical bills. The 2025 tax law raised its limit to $7,500 per household starting in 2026. If you pay for daycare or after-school care, ask your employer about it.

The bottom line

An FSA is a handy way to save on predictable health costs this year. An HSA does that too, plus it can grow into a powerful long-term account. If you're eligible for an HSA, it's one of the best deals in the tax code.

Common questions

What are the HSA contribution limits for 2026?

$4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, plus an extra $1,000 if you're 55 or older.

What is the FSA limit for 2026?

$3,400 per employee for a health FSA. Plans that allow a carryover can let up to $680 roll into 2027.

Can I have an HSA and an FSA at the same time?

Not a general-purpose health FSA. You can pair an HSA with a limited-purpose FSA that covers only dental and vision costs.

What happens to my HSA if I change jobs?

It's yours to keep. The money stays in your account and you can keep using it or investing it.
