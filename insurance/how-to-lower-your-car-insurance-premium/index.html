How to lower your car insurance premium

Full-coverage car insurance now averages well over $2,000 a year. Here are the moves that can lower your bill without leaving you underinsured.

By Jordan NoelNov 17, 20266 min read
The short version
  • Shopping around is the biggest lever. Different insurers can charge very different prices for the same driver.
  • Raising your deductible, bundling policies and asking for every discount can cut your premium further.
  • Don't save money by dropping liability coverage to state minimums. That can cost you far more after a serious accident.

Car insurance has become a major household expense. Insurify's mid-2026 data put the national average for full coverage at about $2,237 a year, and other rate studies come in higher. The good news: many drivers are paying more than they need to. Here's how to bring your bill down.

1. Shop around every year

Insurers price risk differently, so quotes for the same coverage can vary by hundreds or even thousands of dollars. Get at least three quotes:

  • When your policy renews
  • After a life change: moving, marriage, a new car or a ticket dropping off your record
  • If your rate jumps unexpectedly

Compare the exact same coverage limits and deductibles so the comparison is fair. An independent agent can shop several companies at once.

2. Raise your deductible

Your deductible is what you pay out of pocket before collision or comprehensive coverage kicks in. Raising it from $250 or $500 to $1,000 can noticeably lower your premium. Just make sure your emergency fund can cover it.

3. Bundle your policies

Buying auto plus home or renters insurance from the same company often earns a multi-policy discount. Insuring multiple cars on one policy usually helps too.

4. Ask about every discount

Many discounts aren't applied automatically. Ask about:

  • Safe driver or claims-free discounts
  • Good student discounts for young drivers
  • Defensive driving course completion
  • Low-mileage discounts if you drive less than average
  • Paying in full, autopay and paperless billing
  • Safety and anti-theft features on your car
  • Professional, alumni, employer or military affiliations

5. Try usage-based insurance

"Telematics" programs use an app or a plug-in device to track how you drive: speed, hard braking, phone use and time of day. Safe drivers can earn meaningful discounts. If you often drive late at night or brake hard, it may not help, so ask how the program affects your rate before you join.

6. Reconsider coverage on an older car

Collision and comprehensive coverage pay to repair or replace your car. If your car is worth only a few thousand dollars, the cost of that coverage plus your deductible may not be worth it.

Rule of thumb: If your yearly collision and comprehensive premium is more than about 10% of your car's value, and you could afford to replace the car yourself, consider dropping those coverages. Never drop them if you have a loan or lease; your lender will require them.

7. Improve your credit

In most states, insurers can use a credit-based insurance score to set rates. Paying bills on time and keeping credit card balances low can lead to lower premiums over time. (A few states, including California, Hawaii, Massachusetts and Michigan, limit or ban this practice.)

8. Choose your next car carefully

Insurance costs vary widely by model. Cars with high repair costs, high theft rates or lots of horsepower cost more to insure. Get insurance quotes before you buy.

9. Keep a clean record

Tickets and at-fault accidents can raise your rate for three to five years. Safe driving is the best long-term discount there is.

What not to cut

Liability coverage pays for injuries and damage you cause to others. State minimums are often very low. In a serious accident, medical bills and repairs can quickly exceed them, and you could be personally responsible for the rest. Many experts suggest limits of at least 100/300/100 ($100,000 per person, $300,000 per accident for injuries and $100,000 for property damage) if you can afford it.

Also think twice before dropping uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, which protects you if the other driver has too little insurance or none.

Your 30-minute plan

  1. Pull out your current policy's coverage page.
  2. Get three quotes for the same coverage.
  3. Ask each insurer to check every discount you might qualify for.
  4. Price out a higher deductible.
  5. Switch, or ask your current insurer to match the best quote.

The bottom line

You can't control what insurers charge, but you can control how often you shop, which discounts you claim and how you set up your coverage. A half hour of comparison shopping is often the best-paid work you'll do all year.

Common questions

Why is my car insurance so expensive?

Rates depend on your age, driving record, location, car model, coverage and, in most states, a credit-based insurance score. Rates have also risen nationally as repair costs have climbed.

How often should I shop for car insurance?

Every year at renewal, and after big changes like moving, marriage, buying a car or a ticket dropping off your record. Prices for the same coverage vary a lot between insurers.

Does raising my deductible lower my premium?

Yes. Going from $250 or $500 to $1,000 can noticeably lower your premium. Make sure your emergency fund can cover the deductible.

When should I drop full coverage?

Consider dropping collision and comprehensive if their yearly cost is more than about 10% of your car's value and you could afford to replace the car. Never drop them on a financed or leased car.

Average cost figures come from 2026 rate studies and vary by source. Rules on credit-based pricing and coverage requirements vary by state. This article is for education only and isn't insurance advice.
